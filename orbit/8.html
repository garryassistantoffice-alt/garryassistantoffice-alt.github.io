The script

There is a world so far from the Sun that, at midday, its sky is only as bright as the Earth's sky a little after sunset.

Its plains are made of frozen air. Its mountains are made of water ice, as hard as stone. And over it all hangs a thin, high haze... and when the light comes through it from behind, that haze glows blue.

For eighty-five years after it was found, nobody knew any of this. It was a point of light, too small and too far to show a face. Then, on one summer day, a small machine flew past it... and the point became a world.

Slow Orbit. Episode eight... Pluto and the Kuiper Belt.

The story of Pluto is a story of a search. A search for a planet that turned out not to be there, which found, by patient work and some luck, something smaller and stranger instead.

And then a second search, sixty years later, which found that Pluto was not alone at all... that it was only the first one seen of a great, dim crowd of icy worlds, circling in the dark beyond Neptune.

Chapter one. The edge of the light.

Neptune, the farthest of the big planets, lies about thirty times farther from the Sun than the Earth.

Pluto lies farther still. But not at one fixed distance. Its path around the Sun is a long, tilted oval. At its nearest, it comes in to about thirty times the Earth's distance... a little closer, for a time, than Neptune itself. At its farthest, it swings out to nearly fifty times the Earth's distance, more than seven billion kilometres from the Sun.

At that distance, the Sun is no longer a disk. It is a small, fierce star, the brightest in a black sky. Its light reaches Pluto after four or five hours of travel... more than thirty times longer than the eight minutes it takes to reach the Earth.

And that light is thin. Pluto receives something like a thousandth of the sunlight that falls on the Earth... less still at the far end of its path. Enough to see by. Enough to cast clear, black shadows. But not enough to warm anything.

On the ground, the temperature sits somewhere around two hundred and thirty degrees below zero. At that cold, nitrogen... the gas that makes up most of the Earth's air... freezes solid, and lies on the ground as ice.

Pluto is slow. One trip around the Sun takes it two hundred and forty-eight of our years.

It was found in 1930. Since then, it has not yet travelled even once around the Sun. It will finish its first lap since its discovery in the year 2178.

And its path is tilted. The big planets all travel close to one flat plane, like grooves on a single record. Pluto's oval is tipped out of that plane by about seventeen degrees. For part of its year it climbs high above the others. For part, it dips below.

From 1979 to 1999, Pluto was closer to the Sun than Neptune was. For twenty years, the outermost planet was not the outermost.

But the two never meet. Their paths cross, as seen from above, but they are kept apart by a steady rhythm. For every two trips Neptune makes around the Sun, Pluto makes exactly three. Each time Pluto comes in to its nearest point, Neptune is somewhere far away along its own path. The two have kept this beat, it seems, for billions of years.

Chapter two. Planet X.

At the end of the last century but one, there was a wealthy man in Boston named Percival Lowell. He was a businessman, a traveller, a writer. And he was in love with the planet Mars.

In 1894, he built an observatory on a hill in Flagstaff, in Arizona, high up in clear, dry air, far from city lights. From there he studied Mars, and drew the long, straight lines he believed he saw on it. He thought they were canals, built by a dying civilisation. They were not. The lines were a trick of the eye at the very limit of seeing. But the observatory he built was real, and it outlived his ideas.

Lowell had a second great hope. He believed there was another planet beyond Neptune.

The reasoning came from the story of Neptune itself. Neptune had been found in 1846 by working backwards from small errors in the path of Uranus. Now, some astronomers believed, Uranus still did not move quite as it should, even with Neptune counted in. Something else, farther out, might be pulling on it.

Lowell called it Planet X. He worked out where it might be, page after page of arithmetic, and from about 1905 he had his observatory search for it with photographs.

He did not find it. He died in 1916, still believing it was there.

There is a small irony here. Among the plates taken at Lowell's observatory in 1915, Pluto was later found... two faint specks, caught on the glass, and not recognised. Nobody had known which faint speck, among so many thousands, was the one that mattered.

And there is a deeper irony, though nobody could know it for many years. The errors in the path of Uranus were not real. When Voyager 2 flew past Neptune in 1989, it measured Neptune's mass precisely, and found it a little less than astronomers had been using. With the right mass, the errors in Uranus's path melted away. There was no large Planet X pulling on anything.

The search was aimed at something that did not exist. And yet it would find something that did.

After Lowell's death, the search stopped for more than ten years, slowed by a long dispute over his will. When it began again, the observatory had a new telescope, built for this one task: a camera, really, with a lens thirteen inches wide, that could photograph a wide patch of sky in sharp detail.

What it needed now was someone with the patience to use it.

Chapter three. The farm boy.

Clyde Tombaugh was born in 1906, in Illinois, and grew up on farms... first there, and later on the open plains of western Kansas.

He had no money for university. But he loved the sky. As a young man he built his own telescopes, grinding the mirrors by hand. For one, he dug a pit on the farm to work in, because the air underground held a steady temperature, and a steady temperature made for a truer mirror.

In 1928, with a homemade reflector nine inches wide, he made careful drawings of Jupiter and of Mars. He sent them to the Lowell Observatory. He hoped, he later said, only for some advice.

Instead, they offered him a job.

He arrived in Flagstaff in January of 1929, after a long journey by train. He was twenty-two years old. And he was given the work that nobody else wanted.

The method was simple, and very slow. On a clear night, the new telescope would photograph one patch of sky on a large glass plate. A few nights later, it would photograph the very same patch again. Anything that had moved between the two nights... a planet, an asteroid... would sit in a slightly different place on the second plate. Everything else, the countless distant stars, would sit exactly where it had been.

Each pair of plates held tens of thousands of stars. In the thick star fields near the Milky Way, far more.

To compare them, Tombaugh used a machine called a blink comparator. He set the two plates side by side in it, lined them up exactly, and looked through a single eyepiece. Then he flicked between the two... the first plate, the second plate, the first, the second... back and forth, a small patch at a time.

The stars stayed still. Anything that had moved seemed to jump.

He worked through the plates for months. Night after night at the telescope, day after day at the blink comparator. Hundreds of hours with his eye to the glass, crossing each plate slowly, square by square, looking for one small point that did not keep still.

Most of the things that jumped were asteroids, much closer to the Sun, moving quickly. Some were flaws in the plates. Some were variable stars, brightening and fading. Each had to be checked, and set aside.

In January of 1930, the telescope photographed a region of sky in the constellation of the Twins, near a star called Delta Geminorum. Two plates, taken six days apart.

On the afternoon of the eighteenth of February, 1930, Tombaugh set them in the blink comparator, and began to work across them.

He came to a very faint point. Fainter than almost anything he had been looking at.

On the first plate, it was in one place. On the second, it was a short distance away.

He flicked back and forth. And it jumped... a tiny step, back and forth, among the stars that did not move.

It had moved, but not by much. Something close to the Sun would have moved a long way in six days. This had barely shifted. It was far out... beyond Neptune.

He checked it against other plates. He measured it. And then, the story goes, he walked down the hall to the director's office, and said he had found Planet X.

The observatory did not announce it at once. They watched it for weeks, to be sure. And they chose their day.

On the thirteenth of March, 1930, the Lowell Observatory told the world. It was the day that would have been Percival Lowell's seventy-fifth birthday. And it was the same date, the thirteenth of March, on which William Herschel had found Uranus, one hundred and forty-nine years before.

A farm boy from Kansas, twenty-four years old, with no degree, had found the ninth planet.

Chapter four. A name from Oxford.

A new planet needed a name, and suggestions came from everywhere. Hundreds of them, by letter and by telegram.

One came from a breakfast table in Oxford, in England.

On the morning the news reached the English papers, a retired librarian named Falconer Madan, who had worked for many years at the Bodleian Library, read the story aloud to his granddaughter. Her name was Venetia Burney. She was eleven years old.

She knew her myths, and she knew her planets. She had been learning about them at school. And she thought about this new one: a world so far away, so cold, so dark. And she suggested a name. Pluto... the Roman god of the underworld, the ruler of the dark below.

Her grandfather passed the idea to an astronomer friend in Oxford. From there it went to Flagstaff.

The astronomers at Lowell had a long list to choose from. But the name fitted. The other planets carried the names of Roman gods. Pluto was the brother of Jupiter and of Neptune. And the first two letters, P and L, were the initials of Percival Lowell.

On the first of May, 1930, the name was announced. Venetia's grandfather gave her a reward... a five-pound note.

She went on to become a teacher. She lived until 2009, long enough to see the spacecraft launched that would one day fly past the world she had named.

The name had an echo, too. A little later, a cartoon dog appeared in the films of Walt Disney, and he came to be called Pluto as well. And in the 1940s, when a new element was made, the one after neptunium, it was named plutonium... after the planet.

That element would matter, much later, in a way nobody could have guessed. But that comes further along in the story.

Chapter five. The shrinking planet.

From the start, Pluto was a puzzle. It was much too faint.

Lowell's Planet X was meant to be a large world, several times the mass of the Earth, heavy enough to tug on Uranus. But the point on Tombaugh's plates was dim... far dimmer than such a planet ought to look. And even in the largest telescopes, it never showed a disk. It stayed a point.

So, over the decades, the estimates began to fall.

At first, it was thought to be something like the mass of the Earth. Through the 1950s and 1960s, as astronomers measured more carefully, it kept coming down. A tenth of the Earth's mass, by the late 1960s. Then less. Each new measurement made it smaller.

Some astronomers joked that if the trend went on, Pluto would soon disappear altogether.

The answer came in 1978, from a photograph that looked like a mistake.

At the United States Naval Observatory's station in Flagstaff... a few miles from where Pluto was found... an astronomer named James Christy was measuring images of Pluto, to work out its path more precisely. The images were small and blurred. And on some of them, Pluto was not round. It had a bump on one side.

At first, the bump looked like a fault in the photographs. But when he checked other images, the bump moved. It went around Pluto, from one side to the other, again and again, in a steady rhythm of a little over six days.

It was a moon.

Christy proposed the name Charon, after the ferryman of the myths, who carries the souls of the dead across the river into Pluto's kingdom. It suited the underworld. And, it is said, it also echoed the name of his wife, Charlene.

But the moon did more than add a name. Once you can watch a moon go around a world, you can weigh that world. The size of the moon's orbit and the time it takes give the mass directly, with no guesswork.

And Pluto was tiny. Its mass turned out to be about two thousandths of the mass of the Earth. Less than a fifth of the mass of our own Moon.

So it was not Planet X. It could not have pulled on Uranus at all. It was far too small.

Later measurements settled its size. Pluto is about two thousand, three hundred and seventy kilometres wide. Smaller than our Moon. Smaller than several of the moons of Jupiter and Saturn. Laid down on the Earth, it would stretch only about halfway across the United States.

A planet, still, in the books of the time. But a very small one, at the edge of everything.

Chapter six. A double world.

Charon is not a small moon. It is about twelve hundred kilometres wide... more than half the width of Pluto itself.

No other moon in the solar system is so large beside the world it circles. So large that the two do not really go around Pluto. They go around a point between them, out in empty space, above Pluto's surface. Like two dancers, one heavier than the other, turning around a spot between them, holding hands.

And they have locked faces.

Charon goes around once every six days and nine hours or so. Pluto turns on its axis in the same time. So the same side of Pluto always faces Charon, and the same side of Charon always faces Pluto.

If you stood on one side of Pluto, Charon would hang in the sky, never rising, never setting, never moving... a great pale globe, many times wider than the full Moon looks from the Earth. And from the other side of Pluto, you would never see it at all.

The two are close. Only about nineteen and a half thousand kilometres apart. Not so far... only a little farther than half the height at which a satellite hangs over one place on the Earth.

Over the years that followed, more moons were found, all of them small, all of them seen with the Hubble Space Telescope. Two in 2005, named Nix and Hydra... Nix for the goddess of the night, Hydra for the many-headed serpent. Then one in 2011, Kerberos, the dog who guards the gate of the underworld. And one in 2012, Styx, after the river itself.

Five moons in all. Charon, the great companion. And four small, irregular lumps of ice, tumbling around the pair, farther out.

Chapter seven. The third zone.

For sixty years after it was found, Pluto seemed to be alone out there. A lone small planet beyond Neptune, with nothing else around it.

But some astronomers had wondered.

In the 1940s, an Irish astronomer, Kenneth Edgeworth, suggested that the material from which the planets formed did not simply stop at Neptune. Out beyond, he thought, the stuff would have been too thinly spread to gather into a large planet. It might instead have stayed as countless small bodies.

In 1951, the Dutch-American astronomer Gerard Kuiper wrote about a similar disk of small bodies beyond Neptune. He thought it might once have been there, early on, and since been scattered away.

Other astronomers took up the idea, and added to it. Some of the comets that come in from the cold, the short-period comets, seemed to need a source not too far away, close to the flat plane of the planets. A belt beyond Neptune would supply them.

But a belt of small dark ice, billions of kilometres away, would be very hard to see. Each body would be a speck far fainter than Pluto.

In 1987, two astronomers began to look for them. David Jewitt and Jane Luu searched year after year, using the newest electronic cameras, at telescopes in Arizona and in Hawaii. For years, they found nothing.

On the thirtieth of August, 1992, at the 2.2-metre telescope on Mauna Kea, in Hawaii, they found a faint, slow-moving point beyond Neptune.

It was given a catalogue code, as new finds are: 1992 QB1.

It was small, perhaps a hundred and some kilometres across. But it was the first. The first body found beyond Neptune since Pluto and Charon. And within months, they found another. And then, as others joined the search, more and more.

They became known as the Kuiper Belt. It is a wide, thick ring of icy bodies, beginning around Neptune's orbit and reaching out to about fifty times the Earth's distance from the Sun. Thousands have now been found, and astronomers believe there are very many more... perhaps hundreds of thousands larger than a hundred kilometres across, and far more that are smaller.

Leftovers, most of them. Pieces from the time the planets were built, more than four and a half billion years ago, kept in the cold ever since, almost unchanged.

1992 QB1 had to wait a long time for a name. In 2018, it was named Albion, an old name for Britain.

And Pluto, it turned out, was one of them.

Its tilted, stretched path. Its three-to-two beat with Neptune. Its ice. In all of these it matched the new bodies being found. Many of them, in fact, share Pluto's own rhythm with Neptune: three of their orbits for every two of Neptune's. They became known as plutinos... the little Plutos.

Pluto was not a lone planet at the edge. It was the largest known member of a whole family. The first one ever seen.

Chapter eight. The vote.

In the years after 1992, the finds grew larger.

Some of the new bodies were hundreds of kilometres across. Then one was more than a thousand. Each time, the question came a little closer. If one of them was as large as Pluto, what would it be?

The answer came from photographs taken on the twenty-first of October, 2003, at the Palomar Observatory in California, in a survey led by Mike Brown, with Chad Trujillo and David Rabinowitz.

The body on those plates moved so slowly that the search programme missed it. It was only in January of 2005, looking through the old images again, more carefully, that they found it. Far out... nearly a hundred times farther from the Sun than the Earth. And bright, for something so far away. It had to be large.

When it was announced, in July of 2005, the newspapers called it the tenth planet.

It was later named Eris, after the Greek goddess of strife and discord. A fitting name, as it turned out. It has a moon of its own, Dysnomia, the daughter of Eris, the spirit of lawlessness.

Eris is very nearly the same width as Pluto... a little smaller, measurements later showed. But it is heavier, by about a quarter.

So the question could not be put off any longer. If Pluto was a planet, then Eris was a planet. And if more like them were found, as seemed certain, the solar system might have dozens of planets, or hundreds.

Or Pluto was something else.

In August of 2006, the International Astronomical Union, the body that names things in the sky, met in Prague. On the last day of the meeting, the twenty-fourth of August, 2006, the astronomers still in the hall voted on what a planet is.

A planet, they decided, must go around the Sun. It must be heavy enough that its own weight has pulled it into a round shape. And it must have cleared its own path... it must be so much larger than anything else near its orbit that it rules there, alone.

Pluto met the first two. It did not meet the third. It shares its part of the sky with the whole of the Kuiper Belt.

So a new kind of body was named: the dwarf planet. Pluto, Eris, and Ceres, the largest body in the belt of asteroids between Mars and Jupiter, were the first three.

And the solar system, which had had nine planets for seventy-six years, had eight.

Not everyone agreed. Many astronomers, and a great many other people, did not like it. Some of the scientists who study planets argue about it still. In the state of Illinois, where Clyde Tombaugh was born, the legislature passed a resolution declaring that, over their state at least, Pluto was a planet still.

Pluto itself, of course, went on just as it always had. Turning every six days, in step with Charon. Moving along its long, tilted oval, three laps for every two of Neptune's.

And at that moment, a small spacecraft was already on its way to it.

It had been launched seven months before the vote. It had set out for a planet. It would arrive at a dwarf planet.

Chapter nine. The fastest start.

The spacecraft was called New Horizons. It was about the size and shape of a grand piano, with a large dish antenna on one side, pointed back at the Earth.

It was launched from Cape Canaveral, in Florida, on the nineteenth of January, 2006, on top of a large rocket with extra boosters strapped to its sides.

It left faster than any spacecraft ever launched. Apollo crews took about three days to reach the Moon. New Horizons crossed the Moon's orbit in about nine hours.

So far from the Sun, solar panels would be useless. Sunlight at Pluto is far too thin to run a spacecraft. So New Horizons carried its own source of heat: a small amount of plutonium, in a sealed case. As it slowly decays, it gives off heat, and the heat is turned into electricity.

The element named after the planet, carrying a spacecraft to the planet.

A little over a year after launch, at the end of February 2007, it swung past Jupiter. The giant's gravity flung it on faster, and bent its path towards Pluto. On its way past, it photographed Jupiter's storms and its moons, and watched a volcano erupting on the moon Io.

Then came the long cruise. More than eight years. Most of that time, New Horizons was quiet, in hibernation. Its instruments were switched off. Once a week it sent home a simple beacon, a tone, to say that all was well. Once a year or so, it was woken, and checked, and set quiet again.

It passed the orbit of Saturn. Then Uranus. Then Neptune. On and on, into the dark.

It carried a few small things with it. Two American coins. Flags. A piece of the first privately built spacecraft to reach space. A list of names of people who had asked to go.

And, fixed to the upper deck, inside, a small aluminium canister, a few centimetres wide.

Clyde Tombaugh had died in January of 1997, a few weeks before his ninety-first birthday. He had lived to see Charon found, and the first worlds of the Kuiper Belt. He had hoped that a spacecraft might one day visit the planet he had found.

His family gave a small part of his ashes to the mission. They are in that canister. And on it is an inscription, which names him as the discoverer of Pluto and of the solar system's third zone... a son of his parents, a husband and a father, a scientist and teacher, and a friend.

The farm boy who had found the faint point in the blink comparator was going to Pluto.

Chapter ten. The heart.

In the early months of 2015, Pluto began to show itself.

At first, it was a few bright and dark pixels. Week by week, the pixels grew. There were dark patches, and bright ones. Charon, beside it, had a dark, reddish cap at its pole.

And as the spacecraft closed in, during the first days of July, one feature on Pluto grew clearer than the rest. A great, bright, pale region, on one side of the world. It had a shape that no one could fail to see.

It was a heart.

A broad, smooth, pale heart, nearly sixteen hundred kilometres across, laid on the side of a small cold world. It was later named Tombaugh Regio... the Tombaugh region.

On the fourteenth of July, 2015, New Horizons reached Pluto.

It did not stop. It could not. It was moving at about fifty thousand kilometres an hour, and there was no way to slow down enough to stay. It had one chance, one pass, a few hours of close looking after nine and a half years of travel.

At its closest, just before midday by the clock in Greenwich, it passed about twelve and a half thousand kilometres above Pluto's surface.

And during the pass, it was silent.

It did not turn to the Earth to report. Every moment was needed for looking. It turned its cameras and its instruments to Pluto and to Charon, and took everything it could, and stored it.

On the Earth, the team waited. A radio signal from Pluto took about four and a half hours to arrive. All day, they did not know whether the spacecraft had come through.

That evening, at the time it had been told to, New Horizons turned its antenna back towards home, and called. The signal crossed the dark for four and a half hours. And it said the spacecraft was well.

The pictures came slowly. The link from so far away is very weak... it can send only a few thousand bits of data each second. It took more than fifteen months, until late in 2016, for all of the flyby's data to reach the Earth.

But even the first close pictures were enough to show that nearly everything people had expected was wrong.

They had expected an old, dead world. Battered by craters over billions of years. Frozen, unchanging.

Instead, there was the heart.

The left half of it... the western lobe... is a vast plain of ice, about a thousand kilometres across. It is called Sputnik Planitia, after the first satellite to orbit the Earth.

It is not made of water ice. It is made mostly of frozen nitrogen, with some carbon monoxide and methane, the ices of things that are gases here. It lies in a great basin, kilometres deep, perhaps the scar of an ancient impact, now filled.

And on that whole plain, in the sharpest pictures, there is not a single crater.

Craters build up with time. A surface that has none must be young. Astronomers have estimated that the surface of this plain is less than ten million years old, and perhaps much younger. Something is renewing it, all the time.

What renews it is the ice itself.

Seen from above, the plain is broken into shapes, like the cells of a honeycomb, or the cracked mud of a dry riverbed. Each cell is a few tens of kilometres across, bordered by shallow troughs.

Each one is a slow, rising blob of nitrogen ice.

Deep down, the ice is warmed, very slightly, by the heat still left inside Pluto. Warmed ice is lighter. It rises, very slowly, to the top, spreads out, cools, and sinks again at the edges. Like the slow rising and falling of wax in an old lava lamp.

It moves a few centimetres a year. About as fast as a fingernail grows. Slowly enough that you would never see it. Fast enough that, over long ages, the whole surface of the plain is folded under and made new.

And around the edge of the plain, there are glaciers.

On the higher ground to the east, nitrogen frost settles out of the thin air. It gathers, and thickens, and then it flows... down through valleys, slowly, into the great basin. Real glaciers, moving across the face of Pluto. Not of water. Of frozen air.

Along the western shore of the plain, there are mountains.

They rise up in great jumbled blocks, some of them several kilometres high... the tallest about as high as the higher mountains of the Alps. They were named for the first two people to stand on top of the Earth's highest mountain: the Hillary Montes, and the Norgay Montes, after Tenzing Norgay.

They cannot be made of nitrogen ice. Nitrogen ice is too soft, too weak, to hold up mountains. They are made of water ice... which, at two hundred and thirty degrees below zero, is as hard as rock. It is Pluto's bedrock. The mountains seem to be great blocks of it, broken up, perhaps floating, like icebergs, in the softer nitrogen around them.

Farther away from the heart, there is more. Long, dark, reddish lands, stained by something that has fallen from the sky. Plains pocked with old craters. Ranges of tall blades of ice, row upon row, like a frozen forest. And to the south of the heart, two broad mountains with deep pits at their tops, which some scientists suspect may be volcanoes... not of fire, but of slush and ice.

A small cold world, nearly five billion kilometres from the Sun... and it was alive with change.

Chapter eleven. Blue sky.

Pluto has air.

It is very thin. At the surface, the pressure is only about a hundred-thousandth of the pressure at the bottom of the Earth's air. Mostly nitrogen, with a little methane. It comes from the ices on the ground, which turn to gas in what little warmth the Sun brings.

High up in that air, sunlight breaks the methane and the nitrogen apart, and the pieces join up again into larger and larger particles. Soot-like grains, slowly growing, drifting down.

They form a haze. And not just one. New Horizons saw it lying in many thin, separate layers, stacked one above another, rising more than a hundred and fifty kilometres above the surface.

When the particles finally settle to the ground, they are dark and reddish. They are what stains the long dark lands of Pluto, and some think the reddish cap on Charon too, carried across the gap between them.

A day after its closest pass, New Horizons was already far beyond Pluto. And it turned to look back.

From there, Pluto was in front of the Sun. The Sun was hidden behind it. Pluto was a black disk... and around its edge, lit from behind, the haze glowed.

It glowed blue.

The particles in the haze are very small, small enough to scatter the blue part of sunlight more than the red. The same reason, in a different way, that our own sky is blue.

So the picture showed a dark world, ringed with a thin, delicate halo of blue. A sky, at the edge of the solar system, on a world where the Sun is only a star.

And the same picture showed the ground beneath, lit faintly by the light that comes through the haze from behind. Long shadows from the mountains. Mist, perhaps, low in the valleys.

Beyond the flyby, the seasons go on. Pluto is moving outward now, away from the Sun, towards the far end of its oval. In the decades to come, it will grow colder still. Some of its air may freeze out and fall as frost onto the ground. Its sky may grow thinner. And then, a century or so from now, as it swings back in, the frost may rise again.

A world whose air rises and falls back to the ground, once every two hundred and forty-eight years.

Chapter twelve. The beyond.

New Horizons did not stop at Pluto. It could not. And it still had fuel to steer with.

So, before it reached Pluto, the team searched for one more target... a small body of the Kuiper Belt that the spacecraft could reach, farther on. Large telescopes on the ground could not find one close enough to its path. In 2014, the Hubble Space Telescope found one.

It was tiny. Too small, at first, to be anything more than a single faint point, even for Hubble. It sat far out in the belt, on a quiet, nearly circular path, about forty-four times the Earth's distance from the Sun.

New Horizons changed course for it in the autumn of 2015. And it travelled on for more than three more years.

On the first of January, 2019, in the first hours of the new year by the clock in Washington, New Horizons passed it at about three and a half thousand kilometres. Some six and a half billion kilometres from the Sun.

It was, and still is, the most distant world ever visited.

When the pictures came, they showed something like a snowman, lying on its side, or two pebbles stuck together.

Two rounded lobes, one larger, one smaller, joined at a narrow neck. About thirty-six kilometres long from end to end. Deep red, darker and redder than Pluto, with a few brighter patches.

And it had been made gently. The two lobes, it seems, formed side by side, circling each other, long ago. Over time they drew closer, until they touched, slowly... at about the speed of someone walking... and stayed together. No great crash. No shattering. Just a meeting.

That gentleness is what makes it precious. Since the earliest days of the solar system, more than four and a half billion years ago, very little has happened to it. Out there, there is almost nothing to strike it, and almost no warmth to change it. It has kept its shape from the time when the planets themselves were being built, from pebbles and dust.

To look at it is to look at one of the building blocks. Kept in the cold, almost untouched.

It was named Arrokoth, a word from the Powhatan language of the people who lived around the Chesapeake Bay, on the coast of Virginia and Maryland... where the mission was run, and where the Hubble telescope is operated. The word means sky.

And New Horizons is still out there.

After Arrokoth, it went on outward through the belt, looking from afar at other small, icy bodies, measuring the dust and the faint glow of space around it. It is now more than sixty times farther from the Sun than the Earth is, moving outward at about fourteen kilometres every second. Its signals take more than eight hours to come home.

And on its upper deck, in his small canister, Clyde Tombaugh goes with it.

Chapter thirteen. Pluto.

From the Earth, Pluto cannot be seen without a large telescope. Even then, it is only a faint point, one among thousands, moving so slowly that it takes a whole night of careful looking to notice it has moved at all.

But on that faint point, it is daytime somewhere.

The Sun is a small, bright star, low over the mountains of water ice. The light is a thin, cold, steady light, like the last light of a winter evening. On the great plain of the heart, the cells of nitrogen ice are rising, and turning, a few centimetres a year. On the high ground to the east, frost is settling, and gathering, and slipping down the valleys into the plain.

High above, the layers of haze drift in the thin air. Grains of dark dust are falling through them, very slowly, towards the ground.

And over one side of Pluto, Charon hangs in the sky. It does not rise. It does not set. A great pale globe, half lit, always in the same place, as it has been for longer than there have been people on the Earth to see anything at all.

Beyond Pluto, the belt goes on. Thousands upon thousands of small icy bodies, scattered through the dark. Most have never been seen. Each one goes around the Sun on its own slow path, once every two or three centuries. Leftovers from the beginning, kept in the cold.

There is a world so far from the Sun that, at midday, its sky is only as bright as the Earth's sky a little after sunset.

A farm boy found it, with two plates of glass and a great deal of patience. A girl at a breakfast table named it. A small machine, carrying a little of the farm boy with it, went out to see it, and passed on, into the belt beyond.

And tonight, far out past Neptune, in the faint light of a distant star, Pluto and Charon are turning still... face to face, as they always have.

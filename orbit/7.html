The script

There is a planet that rolls around the Sun on its side.

Its north pole points almost along its path. For one long stretch of its year, the Sun stands over one pole and never sets. For another long stretch, that same pole lies in darkness, for more than forty years at a time.

And beyond it, farther out still, there is a second blue world... one that was found not by a telescope, but by a man at a desk, working through pages of arithmetic, who told astronomers exactly where to look.

Slow Orbit. Lecture seven... Uranus and Neptune, the ice giants.

Almost everything we have seen of these two planets up close, we owe to one small machine. It flew past each of them once, a few hours of close looking after years of travel, and then it went on its way and never came back. No spacecraft has been there since.

That machine was Voyager 2. It passes through the middle of tonight's lecture. But the story begins much earlier, in a back garden in England, with a musician.

Chapter one. The far cold.

Past Saturn, the solar system grows very wide and very quiet.

Saturn lies about nine and a half times farther from the Sun than the Earth. Uranus lies about twice as far again... around nineteen times the Earth's distance, nearly three billion kilometres out. And Neptune lies about thirty times the Earth's distance... some four and a half billion kilometres from the Sun.

Light, which crosses from the Sun to the Earth in a little over eight minutes, takes more than two and a half hours to reach Uranus. It takes about four hours to reach Neptune. A ray of morning sunlight that touches the Earth at breakfast would only be arriving at Neptune around lunchtime.

From Neptune, the Sun is no longer a disk. It is a point... the brightest star in a black sky. It gives about one nine-hundredth of the light that falls on the Earth. Still far more than the full Moon gives us. But a thin, white, distant light, with no warmth in it.

At those distances, everything is slow. Uranus takes eighty-four of our years to travel once around the Sun. Neptune takes about one hundred and sixty-five.

A person born when Uranus stood in one constellation would be an old man or woman before it returned there. And Neptune, in all the time since it was first seen, has gone around the Sun only once.

The two planets are very nearly twins in size. Each is about four times as wide as the Earth. Uranus is a little larger across. Neptune is a little smaller, but heavier... about seventeen times the mass of the Earth, against Uranus's fourteen and a half.

Both are blue-green. Both are wrapped in deep, cold air. Both carry thin, dark rings, and families of moons.

And for most of human history, nobody knew that either of them was there.

Chapter two. A musician's comet.

On a clear night, from a dark place, Uranus can just be seen with the naked eye. It is a very faint, steady point, near the limit of what the eye can find. If you knew exactly where to look, you might catch it. But nobody did know. It looked like one more faint star among thousands.

In fact, it had been seen. In 1690, the first Astronomer Royal of England, John Flamsteed, wrote it down in his catalogue of stars, and gave it a number, as if it were a star in the constellation of the Bull. Over the next century, other astronomers noted it too. Each time, they took it for a star. Each time, it had moved on before anyone looked again.

The man who finally saw it for what it was had not set out to be an astronomer at all.

William Herschel was born in Hanover, in Germany, in 1738. As a young man he played the oboe in a military band. He came to England, and made his living as a musician... teaching, composing, conducting, playing the organ. In 1766 he became the organist of the Octagon Chapel, a fashionable chapel in the city of Bath.

But at night he read about the stars. And then he began to build telescopes. Shop telescopes were too expensive, and too small, so he made his own... grinding and polishing the metal mirrors himself, for hours at a time. His sister Caroline worked beside him. In her memoir, she wrote that she would sometimes feed him while he polished, so that he would not have to stop.

On the night of the thirteenth of March, 1781, in the garden behind his house in New King Street, in Bath, he was sweeping the sky with a telescope he had built himself, its mirror a little over six inches wide.

Among the stars near the border between the Bull and the Twins, he noticed something that did not look like a star.

A star, however much you magnify it, stays a point. This one did not. When Herschel used a stronger eyepiece, it grew a little larger... a tiny, pale disk. And over the following nights, it moved, slowly, against the stars around it.

He thought it must be a comet. That is what he reported, to the Royal Society, in the spring of that year. A curious object... perhaps a nebulous star, perhaps a comet.

But it was not behaving like a comet. It had no tail, and no fuzzy head. And as other astronomers worked out its path, the path did not stretch away into a long, thin ellipse, the way a comet's does. It was nearly a circle. And it lay far out, beyond Saturn.

Within a few months, it was clear. Herschel had found a planet.

It was the first planet discovered in all of recorded history. Every other one, from Mercury to Saturn, had been known since before there was writing. In one night, a musician in a back garden had doubled the size of the known solar system.

Then came the question of a name.

Herschel wanted to honour the king who had become his patron, King George the Third. He called the new planet Georgium Sidus... the Georgian Star. The king gave him a pension, and Herschel gave up music, and spent the rest of his life on the stars.

Outside Britain, the name was not popular. A planet named after the King of England did not sit well in France, or in the new United States. Some French astronomers simply called it Herschel.

A German astronomer, Johann Elert Bode, suggested something else. All the other planets carried the names of the old gods, he said, so this one should too. Saturn was the father of Jupiter. So the new planet, beyond Saturn, should be named after Saturn's father... Uranus, Ouranos in Greek, the god of the sky itself.

Grandfather, father and son. Uranus, Saturn, Jupiter, side by side in the heavens.

Most of Europe soon took up Bode's name. But in Britain, the official almanac for sailors went on calling the planet the Georgian Star for nearly seventy years. Only in 1850 did it give way, and use Uranus like everyone else.

Six years after the planet, Herschel found two of its moons. He was by then building far larger telescopes, and in January of 1787 he saw two faint points circling the planet. They were left without names for more than half a century. We will come back to the names they were finally given. They came from a theatre.

Chapter three. The planet found with a pen.

Once Uranus was known to be a planet, astronomers could look back through the old star catalogues, find the times it had been noted down as a star, and use them to work out its path very precisely.

And when they did, something was wrong.

By the 1820s, the tables of its motion were not working. A French astronomer, Alexis Bouvard, drew up the best tables he could, using all the observations, old and new. Within a few years, Uranus had drifted away from them. First it ran a little ahead of where it should be. Then, slowly, it fell behind.

The errors were tiny... a few hundredths of a degree. But astronomers by then could measure the sky very finely, and the errors kept growing.

What could be pulling it?

One answer was that the law of gravity itself might be slightly wrong, so far from the Sun. Another, which Bouvard himself suspected, was that something unseen, farther out still, was tugging at Uranus as it passed. Another planet.

To find it, someone would have to turn the problem around. Not work out how a known planet pulls on Uranus, but start from the small errors in Uranus's path and work backwards... to where an unknown planet would have to be, and how heavy, to cause them.

It was an enormous calculation, all done by hand. Two men, in two countries, took it on.

In England, a young mathematician at Cambridge, John Couch Adams, began working on it as a student. By the autumn of 1845, he had a result... a predicted place in the sky. He went to see the Astronomer Royal, George Airy, at Greenwich, but did not find him in. Messages went back and forth. Airy asked a question. Adams did not answer it. Nothing much was done.

In France, at the same time, a mathematician named Urbain Le Verrier worked through the same problem, independently, at the request of the director of the Paris Observatory, François Arago. In the summer of 1846 he published his answer: where the new planet should be found, and how bright it ought to look.

Now, at last, the English began to search. At Cambridge, the astronomer James Challis set out to map every star in the region Adams had given. He had no up-to-date star chart for that part of the sky, so he had to measure each star, night after night, and compare. Later it was found that he had seen the new planet more than once, that summer, and written it down. But he had not yet compared his notes. He had not yet noticed.

Le Verrier, meanwhile, could not get the astronomers of Paris to look.

So in September of 1846 he wrote to a young astronomer in Berlin named Johann Gottfried Galle. Look here, he wrote... at a certain point in the constellation of Aquarius, the water-bearer. You should find a planet with a small, round disk.

The letter arrived on the morning of the twenty-third of September.

That same day, Galle asked the director of the Berlin Observatory, Johann Encke, for permission to use the large telescope. The story goes that it was Encke's birthday, and that he said yes, and went home to his party.

That night, Galle went up to the dome with a student named Heinrich d'Arrest. d'Arrest had an idea: there was a new, detailed chart of that exact part of the sky, just printed, at the observatory. They could compare.

So Galle sat at the telescope, and called out the positions of the stars he saw, one after another. And d'Arrest sat at a table with the chart, checking each one.

At some point, a little after midnight, Galle called out a star of the eighth magnitude.

And d'Arrest looked at the chart, and said: that star is not on the map.

It was within about one degree of the place Le Verrier had predicted. Less than twice the width of the full Moon. The next night, they looked again, and it had moved, just a little, exactly as a planet should.

Galle wrote back to Paris. The planet whose place you have pointed out, he said, really exists.

The news crossed Europe in days. Arago said that Le Verrier had found a planet with the point of his pen. It was a new kind of discovery: a world found first by mathematics, and only afterwards by the eye.

Then the arguments began. When it became known that Adams had made a similar prediction, months earlier, in England, there was a long and bitter quarrel, partly between two nations, about who deserved the credit. Historians still turn over the old letters today, weighing how close Adams's answer really was, and how firmly he had given it.

The name was settled within months. Neptune... the god of the sea. A fitting name for a deep blue world, though no one at the time had any way of knowing its colour.

And it turned out that Neptune, too, had been seen before. More than two centuries earlier, in December of 1612 and January of 1613, Galileo had been watching the moons of Jupiter. Neptune happened to be passing close by, and he drew it in his notes as a star. By chance, it was then standing almost still in the sky, at the turning point of its path... the one moment when it was hardest to see it move.

Seventeen days after Galle's discovery, in October of 1846, an English amateur astronomer named William Lassell... a brewer by trade, from Liverpool, with a fine telescope of his own... turned it on the new planet, and found a large moon going around it.

That moon is Triton. It has its own chapter tonight.

Neptune is so slow that it was only in July of 2011 that it came back, for the first time, to the place in the sky where Galle and d'Arrest first found it. One Neptune year, since the night someone said: that star is not on the map.

Chapter four. What an ice giant is.

Jupiter and Saturn are called gas giants. They are made almost entirely of hydrogen and helium, the lightest substances there are... nearly the same mixture as the Sun.

Uranus and Neptune are different. They are smaller, and they are made mostly of something else. Astronomers call them ice giants.

It is not the ice of a frozen pond. To an astronomer, ices are substances like water, ammonia and methane... things that freeze solid in the cold of the outer solar system.

Inside the planets now, it is not frozen at all. It is hot, and squeezed under enormous pressure, into a thick, dense, slushy fluid... something between a liquid and a solid, with no exact match on Earth. Above it lies a deep atmosphere of hydrogen and helium. Below it, perhaps, a core of rock.

The colour comes from the methane.

High in their atmospheres, methane gas soaks up the red part of sunlight. The light that bounces back out of the clouds has lost its red, and what remains is blue and green.

For a long time, pictures showed Neptune a deep, rich blue. But in 2024, a team at Oxford went back to the original measurements and worked out the true colours as an eye would see them. Neptune's blue had been strengthened in the early pictures. In truth, the two planets are much the same: a soft, pale, greenish blue.

Deep inside, there may be something stranger still.

Some scientists think that, far down, where the heat and pressure are immense, the methane could break apart. Its carbon would be squeezed together into diamond. Small crystals of diamond might form, and sink, slowly, through the dense fluid, toward the centre of the planet.

It is called diamond rain. It is a hypothesis... an idea that has not been seen. Nobody can look that deep. But in 2017, scientists in California fired powerful lasers at a plastic made of carbon and hydrogen, squeezing it for a tiny fraction of a second. For that instant, tiny diamonds formed.

So diamond rain is possible. Whether it really falls, inside Uranus and Neptune, is still an open question.

There is one more difference between the twins. It has to do with heat.

Jupiter, Saturn and Neptune all give out more heat than they receive from the Sun. They are still slowly releasing the warmth of their making. Neptune gives off more than twice as much energy as the faint sunlight brings it. That inner heat stirs its atmosphere from below.

Uranus gives off very little of its own, and nobody is sure why. It may have lost its early heat long ago, or the heat may be trapped deep inside.

So Uranus, though it is closer to the Sun, holds a strange record. In its upper air, it has the coldest temperatures measured on any planet... around two hundred and twenty-four degrees below zero.

Chapter five. Rolling on its side.

Most planets spin more or less upright, as they travel around the Sun. The Earth leans a little... about twenty-three degrees. That lean gives us our seasons.

Uranus leans about ninety-eight degrees.

It is tipped right over. Its poles point, more or less, along the line of its path around the Sun. And it is tipped just past the sideways mark, so that, strictly speaking, it spins the other way from most of the planets. If the Earth turns like a spinning top, Uranus turns like a barrel rolling along the ground.

Its rings and larger moons are tipped over with it, so that at times the whole system looks like the face of a clock, seen front on.

The seasons that follow from this are like nothing on Earth.

As Uranus travels around the Sun, first one pole faces the Sun, and then, a quarter of its year later, the equator, and then the other pole. Near each pole, the Sun stays up for about forty-two years without setting. Then, for about forty-two years, there is night.

In between come the turning seasons, when the Sun stands over the equator and days and nights pass as they do anywhere else. The last time was in 2007. Now the north pole is moving into its long summer.

How did a whole planet come to lie on its side?

Nobody knows for certain. The most widely held idea is that, very early in its life, something enormous struck it... another young world, perhaps one or two times as heavy as the Earth... and knocked it over. Some studies suggest it took more than one blow. Others propose quieter ways, over long ages, through the slow pull of other planets, or of a large moon that is no longer there.

It is a question that may only be answered by going back.

Even the length of its day was uncertain until very recently.

Uranus has no solid surface, and few markings to follow as it turns. Voyager 2's measurement, in 1986, was not quite exact enough to last.

In 2025, astronomers using the Hubble Space Telescope published a new measurement. For more than ten years, they had followed the planet's faint ultraviolet auroras as they were carried around by its turning. A day on Uranus, they found, lasts seventeen hours, fourteen minutes and fifty-two seconds.

Twenty-eight seconds longer than anyone had thought.

Chapter six. The rings in the dark.

Saturn's rings can be seen with a small telescope. Uranus's rings, nobody had ever seen. Nobody was looking for them.

In March of 1977, a team of American astronomers led by James Elliot set out to watch Uranus pass in front of a faint star. The way the starlight dimmed through Uranus's upper air would tell them about that air. They watched from a flying observatory, an aircraft carrying a telescope, out over the Indian Ocean.

On the tenth of March, the star was still some way from the edge of the planet when, quite unexpectedly, it winked out. Just for a moment. Then it came back. Then it winked again. And again. Five times, before the planet ever reached it.

And after the star came out from behind the planet on the other side, it winked again... the same pattern, in reverse.

Something was circling Uranus, on both sides, at the same distances. Something too dark, and too thin, to be seen... but solid enough to block a star.

Rings.

They are nothing like Saturn's broad sheets of bright ice. Uranus's rings are narrow and dark... as dark as charcoal, or soot. Most of them are only a few kilometres wide. The broadest is little more than a hundred. Thirteen of them are known today.

They had been there all along. It took the light of a far star, passing behind them, to show that they were there.

Neptune has rings too. They are fainter still... thin, dusty, and dim. In the 1980s, passing stars seemed to wink on one side of the planet but not the other, as if the rings were only arcs. Voyager 2 showed that they go all the way around, faint almost everywhere, with a few bright clumps in the outermost ring, where the dust is gathered thick.

The rings of Neptune are named after the people who found the planet: Galle, Le Verrier, Lassell, Arago, and, outermost, Adams. The long quarrel of 1846 ended, after all, in a circle of dust, all of them together.

And the bright arcs in the Adams ring were given French names, in honour of Le Verrier's country: Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité... liberty, equality, fraternity... and one more, called Courage.

They do not last. Seen from the Earth over the years since Voyager, the arcs have been changing. By 2009, Liberté and Courage had faded almost to nothing. Égalité and Fraternité remain.

Chapter seven. The long road.

In the late 1970s, the outer planets lined up in a way that comes around only once in about a hundred and seventy-five years. A spacecraft sent at the right moment could swing past each planet in turn, and be flung by its gravity on to the next.

Voyager 2 left Florida on the twentieth of August, 1977... a small machine of aluminium, wire and gold foil, with a large white dish to talk to the Earth. It passed Jupiter in 1979 and Saturn in 1981. Then it went on alone, for four and a half more years, outward into the dark.

On the twenty-fourth of January, 1986, Voyager 2 swept past Uranus, about eighty-one thousand kilometres above its clouds.

Its signals took nearly three hours to reach home.

What it saw, at first, was very little. A smooth, pale, blue-green ball, almost without a mark. But around the planet it found ten new moons, small and dark, and two new rings.

And it found that Uranus's magnetic field is strange. On the Earth, a compass points nearly north. On Uranus, the magnetic field is tilted about sixty degrees from the axis the planet spins on, and it does not even start at the centre. It is pushed off to one side, about a third of the way to the surface... as if a bar magnet had been put inside the planet crooked.

For nearly forty years, everything we knew about the space around Uranus came from those few days. Then, in 2024, scientists looking again at the old measurements noticed that, just before Voyager arrived, the wind of particles from the Sun had been unusually strong. It had squeezed the magnetic bubble around Uranus far smaller than usual.

Voyager had arrived in one of the strangest weeks of the planet's year. One flyby is one moment. That is why scientists want to go back.

Chapter eight. Moons from the theatre.

Herschel's two moons waited sixty-five years for their names. By then, Lassell, the brewer from Liverpool, had found two more. In 1852, Herschel's son, the astronomer John Herschel, gave all four their names. He took them not from the old gods, but from English poetry.

Titania and Oberon are the queen and king of the fairies in Shakespeare's A Midsummer Night's Dream. Ariel and Umbriel come from a playful poem by Alexander Pope, The Rape of the Lock... a bright, airy spirit, and a dusky, melancholy sprite. In 1948, Gerard Kuiper found a fifth moon and kept to the custom. He called it Miranda, after the magician's daughter in The Tempest.

And so it has gone on. Juliet, Desdemona, Ophelia, Portia, Cressida, Puck... every moon of Uranus is named from Shakespeare, or from Pope. Twenty-nine are known. The newest, about ten kilometres across, was found in pictures from the James Webb Space Telescope in 2025, and is still waiting for its name.

Of all of them, the strangest is Miranda.

It is less than five hundred kilometres across, and it looks as though it had been broken apart and put back together wrongly. Old, cratered ground lies beside huge patches of grooves and ridges, like the tracks of a racecourse, or a ploughed field.

And on Miranda there is a cliff called Verona Rupes, named after the city of Romeo and Juliet. By recent estimates, it is about twenty kilometres high... more than twice the height of Everest. It may be the tallest cliff in the solar system. Miranda's gravity is so weak that a stone dropped from the top would take many minutes to reach the bottom.

The others each have their own face. Ariel is the brightest, with long valleys across it. Umbriel, true to its name, is the darkest... old and grey, with one strange bright ring near its pole, like a pale coin on a dark floor. Titania is the largest, crossed by great canyons. And Oberon, the farthest, is old and deeply cratered.

Chapter nine. The windiest world.

After Uranus, Voyager 2 had one planet left. It travelled on for three and a half more years. Out there, the sunlight is less than half as bright as at Uranus.

On the twenty-fifth of August, 1989, it reached Neptune.

It flew closer to Neptune than to any other planet of the whole journey... about five thousand kilometres above the clouds of its north pole, low enough to bend its path sharply around the planet.

Its radio signals, by then, took about four hours to reach the Earth.

Everyone expected Neptune to be like Uranus... a quiet, pale, almost featureless ball. It was not.

Neptune was alive with weather. Long, bright white clouds, high in the air, cast shadows on the blue deck below.

And Neptune, it turned out, has the fastest winds measured on any planet in the solar system. Near its equator, they blow at around two thousand kilometres an hour... far faster than the strongest hurricane on Earth, faster than the speed of sound in our air. Strangely, they mostly blow against the planet's turning.

How can a planet so far from the Sun, with so little sunlight to drive it, have such winds?

Part of the answer may be its inner heat, stirring the air from below. And part may be that there is no ground to slow the wind. Once the air is moving, it may simply go on.

In the southern clouds, Voyager found a great storm. Astronomers called it the Great Dark Spot.

It was an oval of deep blue, about thirteen thousand kilometres long... about as wide as the Earth. Bright white clouds, like high cirrus, hung along its edges. And one small, bright patch of cloud raced around the planet so quickly that the scientists called it the Scooter.

Five years later, in 1994, the Hubble Space Telescope looked at Neptune from the distance of the Earth. The Great Dark Spot was gone.

Unlike the Great Red Spot on Jupiter, which has lasted for centuries, Neptune's dark storms come and go. Since then, Hubble has watched others form, drift, shrink and fade, over a few years each. In 2018, a new large dark spot appeared in the north, very like the one Voyager saw. Neptune's weather is restless. Its face changes from decade to decade.

Neptune's magnetic field, Voyager found, is crooked too... tilted about forty-seven degrees from its spin, and pushed even farther off-centre than Uranus's, more than halfway from the centre to the surface.

Both ice giants, then, have lopsided magnets. Many scientists think their fields are made not in a deep core, but in the shell of hot, slushy water and ammonia, closer to the surface, where the currents can wander off-centre.

Chapter ten. Triton.

Five hours after it passed over Neptune's north pole, Voyager 2 swept past Neptune's great moon, Triton, at about forty thousand kilometres.

It was the last world it would ever see up close.

Triton is a little smaller than our own Moon... about two thousand seven hundred kilometres across. It is by far the largest of Neptune's sixteen known moons. And it does something no other large moon in the solar system does.

It goes around its planet backwards.

Every other large moon circles its planet in the same direction that the planet spins. Triton circles Neptune the other way, against the turning, on a tilted path.

A moon that formed alongside its planet, out of the same spinning disk of material, would not move like that. So most astronomers think Triton did not form there. It was once a world of its own... a small, icy body from the far, cold region beyond Neptune called the Kuiper Belt, a cousin of Pluto. Long ago, it came too close to Neptune, and was captured.

Voyager's pictures showed a strange, beautiful face.

Much of Triton's surface is frozen nitrogen... the same gas that makes up most of the Earth's air, here frozen hard as rock. At about two hundred and thirty-five degrees below zero, it is one of the coldest surfaces ever measured in the solar system. Parts of it are wrinkled and dimpled, like the skin of a melon. Parts are smooth, frozen plains. Over the south, a broad cap of pinkish ice lay in the sunlight of its long southern summer.

And on that cap, Voyager saw something nobody had expected on a world so cold.

Plumes. Thin, dark columns, rising straight up from the ice, about eight kilometres high. At the top, they bent over, and trailed away sideways for a hundred kilometres or more, carried by the thin wind of Triton's faint nitrogen air, like smoke from a distant chimney on a calm day.

Across the cap lay many dark streaks, all pointing the same way... the marks of earlier plumes, their dust laid down on the ice in long lines.

What drives them is still not settled. One idea is that weak sunlight shines through clear nitrogen ice and warms dark material beneath it, until the gas bursts through. Another is that the heat comes from inside Triton itself.

Either way, on one of the coldest surfaces anyone has seen, there is movement. Something is still rising, slowly, into the dark.

Triton's backward orbit is slowly bringing it closer. The tides it raises on Neptune, pulling against its motion, drag it little by little inward. Very far in the future, perhaps billions of years from now, it may come so close that Neptune's gravity pulls it apart.

Then its pieces would spread out into a ring around Neptune... perhaps a broad, bright ring, like Saturn's.

But that is a story for another night, and a very long night indeed.

Chapter eleven. Seen again.

Since Voyager, no spacecraft has visited either ice giant. Everything new has come from far away, from telescopes.

The Hubble Space Telescope has watched both planets for more than thirty years, season by season. It has watched Uranus slowly turn its north pole toward the Sun.

And since 2022, there has been another eye.

The James Webb Space Telescope sees in infrared light... light a little too red for our eyes. In it, the ice giants no longer look blue, but pale, almost white.

In September of 2022, Webb's first picture of Neptune was released. For the first time since Voyager, its faint rings could be seen clearly... thin, bright circles, and fainter bands of dust. Beside the planet, Triton shone so brightly, from its frozen nitrogen, that in the picture it looked like a star, with long points of light around it.

In 2023, Webb turned to Uranus. In its pictures, the planet glowed with a soft white cap over its north pole, the cap that forms in the long summer, and a bright storm at the cap's edge. And around it, the dark rings, which had taken a passing star to find, now showed plainly... eleven of the thirteen, fine lines of light circling the planet.

And in 2025, astronomers announced that Webb had seen auroras on Neptune... glowing light high in its air, where charged particles rain down along its crooked magnetic field. Hints of them had been caught by Voyager, but this was the first clear sight.

Because Neptune's magnetic field is tilted so far, its auroras do not glow at its poles, the way ours do. They glow much nearer to its middle.

The same measurements showed something else. The very top of Neptune's atmosphere is colder now than it was when Voyager passed... by several hundred degrees. Why, nobody can yet say.

Chapter twelve. The ones not yet sent.

Scientists very much want to go back.

In 2022, a large committee of American planetary scientists, looking ten years ahead, named their first choice for a new large mission: an orbiter to circle Uranus for years, carrying a probe to drop into its air.

It is a proposal, not a spacecraft. If it is ever built, the journey alone would take more than ten years, and it would arrive, at the very soonest, in the 2040s.

There have been proposals for Neptune too. One, called Trident, would have flown past Triton to look at its plumes. In 2021 it was one of the finalists in a NASA competition, but it was not chosen.

So for now, the ice giants wait. A Uranus summer is long. A Neptune year is longer.

And Voyager 2 is still out there.

After Neptune, it kept going. In November of 2018, it crossed out of the great bubble of the Sun's own wind, into the space between the stars. It is now more than a hundred times farther from the Sun than the Earth is, still travelling outward, at more than fifty thousand kilometres an hour.

It carries, like its twin, a golden record... sounds and pictures of the Earth, and music, in case anyone ever finds it.

It will not come near another star for tens of thousands of years. But for a few days, long ago, it was close to two blue worlds that no one had ever seen, and that no one has seen so closely since.

Chapter thirteen. The ice giants.

From the Earth, Uranus is a faint point, almost too dim to see. Neptune cannot be seen without a telescope at all. On a clear night, they are both up there somewhere, among the stars, moving so slowly that a whole human life passes while they make a single lap of the sky.

But on those faint points, there is weather. On Neptune, winds faster than sound are blowing across a whole world, and somewhere in its clouds, a dark storm may be forming, or fading. On Triton, a thin plume of dust may be rising into the black, and bending over in the faint wind, and drifting away.

Around Uranus, the dark rings circle, and the moons with their names from the stage... Titania and Oberon, Ariel and Umbriel, Miranda with her great cliff... go around in their slow, tilted rounds. Deep inside both planets, if the idea is right, small diamonds may be falling, slowly, through the dark.

If you could stand on Miranda, Uranus would hang enormous in its sky... a vast, soft, pale blue-green globe, many times wider than the Moon looks from the Earth. Its thin rings would cross in front of it, as fine as the lines of a drawing. And the Sun, tiny and white, would shine on it from the side, low and steady, season after season.

There is a planet that rolls around the Sun on its side.

Its poles take turns in the light, forty-two years each, while the rings and moons wheel around it like the face of a clock. And beyond it, a second blue world, found with a pen before it was ever seen, goes on through its long, windy year.

A musician found the first. A man at a desk found the second. A small machine went out to see them both, and passed on.

And tonight, far out beyond Saturn, in the thin light of a distant Sun, they are turning still.

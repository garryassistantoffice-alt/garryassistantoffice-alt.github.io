The script

Every morning, a little after the light comes up, the air begins to warm. Puddles shrink. Leaves turn their faces. The sea, very slowly, gives up a little of itself into the sky.

All of it is the work of one star.

It is not a large star, as stars go. It is not a rare one. There are hundreds of billions like it in our galaxy alone. But it is the only one close enough to have a face... and everything that has ever lived on the Earth has lived in its light.

Slow Orbit. Episode nine... The Sun.

The Sun is so familiar that it is easy to forget what it is. A ball of gas more than a hundred times wider than the Earth, hanging in the dark, burning without fire, and steady... steady enough that life has had time to begin, and grow, and look back up at it.

Tonight we go towards it. Through its light, into its weather, and down, as far as anyone can go, to the place where its light is made.

Chapter one. The nearest star.

The Sun is about a hundred and fifty million kilometres away.

That distance is so useful that astronomers give it its own name. They call it one astronomical unit, and they measure much of the Solar System with it. Mars is about one and a half of those units from the Sun. Neptune, about thirty.

Light, the fastest thing there is, needs about eight minutes and twenty seconds to cross that gap. So the Sun we see is never quite the Sun as it is. It is the Sun as it was, a little more than eight minutes ago. If it went dark this moment, the sky would stay bright for eight more minutes, and nobody on the Earth would know.

It is big in a way that is hard to hold in the mind. Across its middle, the Sun measures about one million, four hundred thousand kilometres. A hundred and nine Earths could sit side by side across its face. And if it were hollow, it could hold about a million Earths inside it.

It is heavy, too. The Sun holds almost all of the matter in the Solar System... a little more than ninety-nine point eight parts in every hundred. Jupiter, the planets, the moons, the comets and the dust, all together, are the crumbs left over.

That weight is what keeps them all in their places. Every planet, every moon and every comet goes around the Sun because the Sun is pulling on it, all the time, across all that distance, and has been since the beginning.

Chapter two. How far away?

For most of history, nobody knew how far away the Sun was. Not even roughly.

More than two thousand years ago, a Greek astronomer, Aristarchus of Samos, tried to work it out. He waited for the moment when the Moon is exactly half lit, and measured the angle between the Moon and the Sun in the sky. From that, with simple geometry, he reasoned that the Sun must be about twenty times further away than the Moon... and so, very much larger than it looked.

His method was right. His measurement was not. The angle he needed was too fine for the eye to judge. The true answer is not twenty times further than the Moon, but about four hundred.

By the sixteen hundreds, astronomers knew the shape of the Solar System very well. They knew that Mars was about one and a half times as far from the Sun as the Earth, and Jupiter about five times. What they did not know was the size of the whole thing... the true length of the unit, the distance from the Earth to the Sun, in miles.

The English astronomer Edmond Halley saw a way. Every so often, Venus passes directly across the face of the Sun, a small black dot creeping over the bright disk. If people watched that crossing from places far apart on the Earth, and timed it exactly, the small differences in their timings would give the distance.

The crossings come in pairs, eight years apart, more than a century between the pairs. Halley knew he would not live to see the next ones. He wrote down his method, and left it for those who would.

In seventeen sixty-one and seventeen sixty-nine, they did it. Expeditions went out to Siberia and Hudson Bay, to India and the Cape of Good Hope, to California and the islands of the Pacific. One ship, under a newly made lieutenant named James Cook, sailed all the way to Tahiti, to watch Venus cross the Sun on a June day in seventeen sixty-nine.

It was harder than anyone expected. As Venus touched the edge of the Sun, it seemed to cling there for a moment, a dark drop joined to the edge, which made the exact instant impossible to judge. Observers standing side by side wrote down different times.

But when all the timings from around the world were put together, the answer came out close... about a hundred and fifty million kilometres, give or take a few in every hundred. For the first time, people knew the size of their own Solar System.

Today the distance is known to within a few metres, by bouncing radio signals off the planets and timing the signals of spacecraft. And in twenty twelve, astronomers simply fixed the astronomical unit, by agreement, as an exact number of metres. The question that defeated Aristarchus is now a matter of definition.

Chapter three. A star like many others.

To the eye, the Sun looks yellow, or orange low in the evening sky. That colour is the air's doing. Seen from space, above the air, the Sun is white... a clean, brilliant white, with a little more green-blue in its light than anything else.

Astronomers sort the stars by their light, and they put the Sun in a large, ordinary family. It is a middle-sized star in the long, steady middle of its life. Some stars are many times larger, and hotter, and bluer, and burn through their lives in a few million years. Many more are smaller and redder, and will go on, very quietly, for far longer than the Sun.

The Sun is in between. Not the brightest. Not the dimmest. And that, it turns out, has been a kind of luck. A star like this one is steady for billions of years. Long enough for oceans. Long enough for life.

It is about four and a half billion years old. It formed out of a great cloud of gas and dust, which slowly drew in on itself, and spun, and flattened, and grew hot at its centre. Most of that cloud became the Sun. A little of what was left, spinning around it, became the planets.

And the Sun is not standing still. It carries the whole Solar System with it on a slow circle around the centre of our galaxy, the Milky Way. One full lap takes something like two hundred and thirty million years. The last time the Sun stood where it stands tonight, on that great circle, the first dinosaurs were only just appearing on the Earth.

Chapter four. What the Sun is made of.

For a long time, nobody could say what the Sun was made of. It was too far to reach, too hot to touch. It seemed that the question would never have an answer.

The answer came from the light itself.

When sunlight is spread out through a prism into its colours, the rainbow it makes is not complete. Across it lie hundreds of thin, dark lines, where certain colours are missing. In the eighteen hundreds, scientists learned to read those lines. Each kind of atom takes out its own colours, as exact as a fingerprint. So the dark lines in sunlight are a list... a list of what the Sun's outer layers contain.

In August of eighteen sixty-eight, during an eclipse in India, a French astronomer, Jules Janssen, studied the light at the Sun's edge and saw a bright yellow line. The English astronomer Norman Lockyer studied the same light that same year, and came to think that the line belonged to an element nobody had yet found on the Earth.

He named it after the Greek word for the Sun, helios. Helium.

For more than twenty-five years, helium was known only in the Sun. Then, in eighteen ninety-five, the chemist William Ramsay found it here, coming out of a mineral in his laboratory. An element first discovered in a star, and only afterwards on the ground beneath our feet.

The deeper answer came in nineteen twenty-five, from a young astronomer at Harvard, Cecilia Payne. In her doctoral thesis, she worked out how the dark lines change with heat, and found that the stars, the Sun among them, are made almost entirely of the two lightest elements, hydrogen and helium.

At the time, almost no one believed it. Most astronomers thought the Sun must be made of much the same stuff as the Earth. She was even persuaded to call her own result probably not real. Within a few years, others had found the same thing in other ways, and she was shown to be right.

Today the count is clear. By weight, the Sun is about three quarters hydrogen and about one quarter helium. Everything else... oxygen, carbon, iron, all the rest... comes to only a pinch, a little over one part in a hundred.

Chapter five. The fire that is not a fire.

The Sun gives out an enormous amount of energy, every second, without pause. For a long time, it was a real puzzle where all that energy came from.

If the Sun were a lump of coal, burning, it would have burned out within a few thousand years. In the eighteen hundreds, the physicist William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, thought the Sun might be shining because it was slowly shrinking, its own weight squeezing it and heating it as it fell inward. By that reckoning, the Sun could be some tens of millions of years old.

But the rocks of the Earth, and the slow work of rivers and seas, and the long history of life, all seemed to need far more time than that. Something was missing.

In nineteen twenty, the English astronomer Arthur Eddington suggested what it might be. Perhaps, deep in the Sun, the lightest atoms were being pressed together into heavier ones, and a little matter was being turned into energy as they joined.

Nearly twenty years later, in nineteen thirty-nine, the physicist Hans Bethe worked out how it could be done, step by step. It was one of the great results of his life, and many years later it won him a Nobel Prize.

This is what happens in the Sun's core.

It is the hottest, densest place in the Solar System. The temperature there is about fifteen million degrees. The gas is squeezed so hard by the weight of everything above it that it is many times denser than lead... and yet, at that heat, it still flows like a gas.

In that crush, the centres of hydrogen atoms are moving so fast that, now and then, they get close enough to join. Through a short chain of meetings, four of them become one centre of helium. And the helium weighs a tiny bit less than the four hydrogens that made it.

That missing sliver of weight becomes energy.

It is a tiny amount each time. But it happens all through the core, all the time. Every second, the Sun turns about six hundred million tonnes of hydrogen into helium... and about four million tonnes of matter simply disappear, turned into light and heat.

It sounds like a great deal. It is. And yet the Sun is so large that it has burned this way for four and a half billion years, and has used up only a part of the hydrogen in its core. It has billions of years still to go.

Chapter six. The long way out.

The energy made in the core does not leave at once.

Around the core lies a deep layer where the gas is packed so tightly that light cannot travel through it in a straight line. A bit of light goes a very short way, and is caught, and given off again in some new direction. Then caught again. And given off again. Forward, back, sideways, over and over, like someone trying to cross a crowded square at night.

So the journey outward is a slow wander. Nobody can follow a single piece of light along it, and the estimates vary a great deal. But it is usually said that the energy made in the core takes tens of thousands of years, perhaps far longer, to work its way through.

Nearer the top, the gas changes its way of moving. It begins to churn. Hot gas rises, gives up its heat near the surface, cools, and sinks again, in great slow loops, the way water rolls in a pan on the stove. This outer layer, the churning part, goes down about a third of the way to the centre.

And at last, the light reaches the surface... the thin, glowing layer we see as the face of the Sun. From there, there is nothing to stop it. It flies straight out into space. Eight minutes and twenty seconds later, some of it lands on the Earth, on the sea, on a leaf, on your hand.

So the warmth of an ordinary afternoon has two ages. Eight minutes, for the last part of its journey. And, for the first, a time longer than all of written history.

Chapter seven. The boiling face.

The Sun has no solid ground. What we call its surface is only the place where its gas becomes thin enough for light to escape. Astronomers call it the photosphere... the sphere of light.

Its temperature is about five and a half thousand degrees. Hot enough to melt any rock or metal on the Earth.

Through a good telescope, with the right filters, that face is not smooth at all. It is covered, edge to edge, in small bright grains, each one ringed with a darker line. They look like rice, or the skin of an orange, or the top of a slow-boiling pot.

Each of those grains is the top of one of the rising loops of hot gas from below. Hot gas wells up in the bright middle, spreads out, cools, and sinks down again at the darker edges.

And small is not quite the word. A typical grain is about as wide as a large country on the Earth... a thousand kilometres or more across. Each one lasts only a little while, perhaps ten minutes, perhaps twenty, before it fades and another rises in its place.

Across the whole face of the Sun there are millions of them, at every moment, rising and fading, rising and fading, without rest.

The Sun turns, too, like the planets do. But because it is not solid, it does not turn all of a piece. Near its middle, it goes around in about twenty-five days. Near its poles, it takes more than a month. The gas near the equator slips ahead of the gas near the poles, and the whole star turns at many speeds at once.

Chapter eight. The ringing Sun.

There is another way to see inside the Sun. Not with light. With sound.

In the early nineteen sixties, astronomers watching the Sun's face closely noticed something new. Patches of the surface were rising and falling, gently, over and over, about once every five minutes. Not here and there, at random. Everywhere.

The Sun, it turned out, is ringing.

Sound waves are travelling through its interior all the time, set off by the churning of the gas near the surface. They bounce around inside the star, and the whole Sun vibrates with them, in millions of different notes at once, like a great bell that is being struck, softly, everywhere, without end.

No one can hear them. The space between the Sun and the Earth is almost empty, and sound cannot cross it. And the notes are far too low for any ear... the deepest of them rise and fall only once every few minutes. But when the movements of the surface are recorded, and played back many thousands of times faster, they become a low, endless murmur, like wind in a great hall.

And each note carries news from inside. Waves of different shapes and pitches travel to different depths. By measuring them precisely, scientists can work out how hot the Sun is at each depth, how dense, how fast it is turning, layer by layer, all the way down.

That is how they found the exact bottom of the churning layer. That is how they learned that the Sun's deep interior turns all of a piece, while its outer layers turn at many speeds. And when the puzzle of the missing neutrinos was at its worst, it was the Sun's ringing that showed the inside of the Sun was very nearly as their models said... so the fault had to lie with the neutrinos.

To listen without a break, there are telescopes spread around the world... so that as the Sun sets at one, it is rising at another. And above them, spacecraft that never see the Sun set at all.

Chapter nine. Spots on the Sun.

Sometimes, on that bright face, there are dark marks.

People had glimpsed them for a very long time... through haze, or smoke, or low over the horizon, when the Sun is dim enough to look at for a moment. Chinese astronomers wrote them down more than two thousand years ago.

In the winter of sixteen ten into sixteen eleven, soon after the first telescopes were turned to the sky, several observers began to watch them closely. In England, Thomas Harriot made the first known drawings of sunspots through a telescope. In Italy, Galileo Galilei followed them day after day, and saw them move across the face of the Sun, change shape, and vanish around its edge. The Sun, he saw, was turning. And it was not perfect.

A sunspot is not a hole, and not a cinder. It is a place where the Sun's magnetism has become knotted and strong, so strong that it holds back the rising heat from below. The gas there is cooler than its surroundings by perhaps one and a half thousand degrees. Still blazing, still hotter than anything on the Earth... but darker beside the rest.

Some sunspots are small. Some are many times wider than the Earth.

And they come and go in a slow rhythm. Over about eleven years, the number of spots rises to a peak, falls away to almost none, and then begins to rise again. At each new cycle, the Sun's whole magnetic field turns over, north becoming south, and south becoming north.

There have been strange quiet spells. Between about sixteen forty-five and seventeen fifteen, almost no spots were seen at all for decades. The astronomers who later dug through the old records, Annie and Walter Maunder, gave their name to that long quiet.

Chapter ten. The crown.

Above the Sun's face lies its atmosphere, thin and faint and enormous.

Normally it cannot be seen. The light of the face drowns it out. But in a total eclipse of the Sun, when the Moon covers the bright disk exactly, it appears all at once... a pale, pearly glow around the black circle, with long soft streamers reaching far out into the sky.

This is the corona... the crown.

And it holds one of the strangest puzzles about the Sun. The face of the Sun is about five and a half thousand degrees. But the corona, above it, is far hotter... more than a million degrees, in places a few million.

It is as if you walked away from a fire, and the air grew hotter the further you went.

Nobody yet knows exactly why. The answer seems to lie in the Sun's magnetism... in countless loops and threads of magnetic field, twisting, snapping and joining again, and in waves running up them, carrying energy up from below and letting it go in the thin gas above. Spacecraft are sent close to the Sun, in part, to find out.

Chapter eleven. The wind from the Sun.

The corona is so hot that the Sun cannot hold all of it. Its gas boils away into space, in every direction, all the time.

This is the solar wind. A thin stream of charged particles, flowing outward at hundreds of kilometres every second... from the Sun, past the planets, out beyond Pluto and the icy belt.

In the nineteen fifties, a young American physicist, Eugene Parker, worked out that such a wind must exist. Many scientists did not believe him, and his paper was nearly turned away. A few years later, the first spacecraft to fly out between the planets measured the wind, just as he had said.

The wind blows out a huge bubble around the Solar System. Far beyond Neptune, more than a hundred times further out than the Earth, it slows at last, and meets the thin gas between the stars. The two Voyager spacecraft, launched in nineteen seventy-seven, passed that edge... Voyager one in twenty twelve, Voyager two in twenty eighteen. They are the only things people have made that have left the Sun's wind behind.

Everything inside that bubble, the Earth included, lives inside the Sun's own breath.

Chapter twelve. The storm of eighteen fifty-nine.

On the morning of the first of September, eighteen fifty-nine, an English astronomer named Richard Carrington was in his private observatory near London, drawing sunspots.

He was using his telescope to throw an image of the Sun onto a screen, and he was carefully tracing a large group of spots. Suddenly, within the group, two patches of brilliant white light appeared, brighter than the Sun's own face. They grew, moved a little across the spots, and faded away, all within about five minutes.

He hurried to find someone else to witness it. When he came back, it was almost gone. Another English observer, Richard Hodgson, saw the same brightening that same morning, on his own.

They had seen a solar flare... a sudden release of stored magnetic energy on the Sun. And with it, though they could not know it, a vast cloud of the Sun's gas had been thrown out into space, straight towards the Earth.

It arrived in less than a day.

On the nights that followed, the sky was filled with lights. Glowing curtains of red and green were seen far south of their usual places... over the Caribbean, over Hawaii, over parts of South America. In some places they were so bright that people woke, thinking it was dawn.

And the telegraph went strange. Across Europe and North America, the wires picked up powerful currents from the disturbed Earth. Some operators got sparks from their equipment. Some found that they could unplug their batteries entirely, and still send messages, carried on the current of the storm itself.

It is still counted among the largest storms from the Sun ever recorded. And it is a quiet lesson for our own time, full of wires and satellites. Today, people watch the Sun every hour of the day, so that when a storm is coming, there is at least a little warning.

Chapter thirteen. The lights in the sky.

Not every meeting between the Sun and the Earth is a storm. Most are gentle, and some are beautiful.

The Earth has its own magnetism, reaching out into space around it like an invisible shell. Most of the Sun's wind slides around that shell and goes on past. But near the poles, some of it is led down into the high air. There, the particles strike the thin gas of the upper atmosphere and make it glow, the way the gas glows in a neon sign.

Oxygen, high up, glows green, and higher still, red. Nitrogen gives touches of blue and purple.

These are the northern and southern lights. Curtains and arcs and slow rippling bands, folding over the sky, sixty, a hundred, two hundred kilometres up. Above Norway and Alaska and Iceland, and over the far southern oceans, they come on many clear, dark nights.

And when the Sun is restless, near the peak of its eleven-year rhythm, they spread further. People who have never seen them before go out into their gardens, far from the poles, and find the sky glowing above them... red and green and soft pink, fading and returning, for an hour, and then gone.

Chapter fourteen. The darkened day.

There is one more meeting between the Sun and the Earth, and it is the strangest of all.

By a coincidence that no one has ever explained, and that does not need explaining, the Sun is about four hundred times wider than the Moon, and about four hundred times further away. So in our sky, the two look almost exactly the same size.

And sometimes, the Moon passes exactly in front of the Sun.

In a total eclipse, the light fades over an hour, the way it does before a storm. Shadows grow sharp and strange. The air cools. Birds fall quiet, or go to roost. Then, in the last moments, the last thin edge of the Sun breaks into bright beads along the Moon's rough edge... and is gone.

For a few minutes, there is night in the middle of the day. The brightest stars and planets come out. And around the black Moon, the pale crown of the Sun is shining, which is otherwise never seen at all.

Eclipses have been useful as well as beautiful. On the twenty-ninth of May, nineteen nineteen, two British expeditions photographed the stars around the darkened Sun, one from the island of Príncipe, off the coast of Africa, and one from Sobral, in Brazil. The stars near the Sun's edge appeared very slightly out of place... their light bent as it passed the Sun's great weight, by the amount that Albert Einstein's new theory of gravity had predicted.

When the results were announced that autumn, they made Einstein famous around the world.

The coincidence will not last forever. The Moon is drifting slowly away from the Earth, a few centimetres each year. In the far future, it will look a little too small to cover the Sun completely, and total eclipses will end. For now, and for a very long while yet, we are living in the time when they happen.

Chapter fifteen. Touching the Sun.

For most of history, the Sun could only be watched from far away. In recent years, people have begun to send machines towards it.

Some watch from a distance. One spacecraft, SOHO, has hung in space between the Earth and the Sun since the nineteen nineties, keeping a constant watch on its face and its corona. Another, Solar Orbiter, swings in and out on long loops, taking the closest pictures yet of the Sun's face, and climbing slowly out of the plane of the planets to see its poles.

And one goes much closer. In August of twenty eighteen, the Parker Solar Probe was launched. It was named for Eugene Parker, the man who had foreseen the solar wind... the first spacecraft named for a living person. He was there to watch it go.

Over the years since, it has swung around Venus again and again, using the planet's pull to bring itself ever nearer to the Sun. On Christmas Eve of twenty twenty-four, it passed about six million kilometres above the Sun's face, inside the corona itself, closer than anything people have ever made.

At that moment it was moving at nearly seven hundred thousand kilometres an hour, faster than any machine ever built. It carries a thick heat shield, facing the Sun, which grows nearly a thousand degrees hot on its front. Just behind it, in its shadow, its instruments stay at about the temperature of a room.

Through it all, it has been tasting the wind as it is born, flying through the crown that no one had ever entered before.

Chapter sixteen. The ghost particles.

There is one way to look directly into the Sun's core. Not with light. Light takes too long to come out, and has forgotten where it came from by the time it does.

But each time four hydrogens become helium in the core, the reaction also gives off tiny particles called neutrinos. They are so small and so shy of everything else that they pass straight through the whole Sun without stopping, and fly out into space at nearly the speed of light.

Eight minutes later, they reach the Earth. And they pass straight through the Earth, too.

They are passing through you now. Tens of billions of them through every square centimetre of your skin, every second, by day and by night... because at night, they come up through the ground beneath you, through the whole planet, and keep on going.

To catch even a few of them, people have built great tanks of liquid deep underground, in old mines, far below the reach of other particles from space. In one of the first, in a gold mine in South Dakota in the late nineteen sixties, Raymond Davis counted the few atoms that the Sun's neutrinos had changed in a tank of cleaning fluid. He found only about a third as many as expected. For decades, nobody knew why.

The answer came from more great detectors, in Canada and in Japan, around the turn of the century. The neutrinos were all arriving after all... but on their way, some of them had changed into other kinds, which the first detectors could not see. Davis was given a Nobel Prize for his long watch, and the teams in Japan and Canada shared another.

So the core of the Sun can be watched, after all. Not by its light, which left long ago... but by these faint, quick messengers, which left only eight minutes before.

Chapter seventeen. The light that arrives.

At the top of the Earth's air, in full sunlight, every square metre of ground facing the Sun receives a little more than thirteen hundred watts of power. A little less, by the time the light has come down through the air to the ground.

It is not very much, for a single square metre. But across the whole face of the Earth, it adds up to far more energy than all the people on the planet use... thousands of times more.

That light warms the land, and the land warms the air. Warm air rises, and cool air moves in beneath it, and that is the wind. The light lifts water from the oceans as vapour, and the vapour cools, and falls as rain, and runs down to the sea in rivers. The weather of the whole Earth is the Sun's light, moving things around.

And in every green leaf, a small part of the light is caught, and used to join water and the air's carbon dioxide into sugar. All the food that any animal has ever eaten began that way. And the coal and oil and gas that people burn are the remains of plants and tiny sea creatures that lived on sunlight many millions of years ago... old sunlight, buried, and brought back up.

Chapter eighteen. The long future.

The Sun will not shine the same forever.

Very slowly, as its core fills with helium, it is growing brighter. About a tenth brighter, every billion years. In about a billion years, it will be bright enough to begin to boil away the Earth's oceans. Long before the Sun itself changes, the Earth will have become a very different place.

In about five billion years, the hydrogen in the core will run low. Then the Sun will begin to change in earnest. Its core will shrink and grow hotter, and its outer layers will swell, and cool, and redden. It will become a red giant, hundreds of times its present width, swallowing Mercury and Venus, and perhaps the Earth as well.

After that, it will shed its outer layers gently into space, as a great, glowing shell of gas. What remains will be its core... a small, white-hot ball, about the size of the Earth, but heavier than half the Sun. A white dwarf.

It will make no more energy. It will only cool, very slowly, glowing fainter and fainter, over a span of time far longer than the age of the universe so far.

And the gas it leaves behind will not be wasted. It will drift out into the galaxy, carrying with it a little of the helium and carbon made deep inside it. In time, some of it may be gathered up into new clouds, and new stars, and new planets.

The Sun itself was made that way, out of the remains of older stars. So is the Earth. So, in a way, are we.

Chapter nineteen. The Sun.

Somewhere on the far side of the Earth, it is noon.

The Sun stands high over a sea, white and too bright to look at. On its face, millions of grains of hot gas are rising, and spreading, and sinking again. Across its middle, a few dark spots are drifting slowly from east to west, carried by the turning of the star.

Above them, the pale crown reaches out into space, and the wind pours out of it, past Mercury and Venus, past the Earth and its Moon, out to Pluto and the belt and the edge of the great bubble, where the old Voyagers are travelling on into the dark.

Deep below, in the core, hydrogen is turning into helium, as it has for four and a half billion years. Light made there tonight will spend an age wandering outward. Neutrinos made there tonight will leave at once, and pass through the whole Earth, and through you, before the light has gone a single step.

Every morning, a little after the light comes up, the air begins to warm.

It is the work of one star. An ordinary star, in the long, steady middle of its life... the only one close enough to have a face.

The script

Saturn is lighter than water.

It is a world nearly ten times as wide as the Earth, and ninety-five times as heavy. And yet, for its size, it is so light that if you could find an ocean large enough to hold it, Saturn would not sink.

Around it, in a perfectly flat circle, turn its rings... wide enough to stretch most of the way from the Earth to the Moon, and in most places no thicker than a house is tall.

Slow Orbit. Lecture six... Saturn and its rings.

This lecture is read by a synthetic voice. If these lectures keep you company at night, follow the show... it helps others find it.

Almost everything in this lecture, we learned in the last four hundred years. The first part came through a small telescope in Italy. The last part came from a machine the size of a small bus, which spent thirteen years going around Saturn, and then, on purpose, fell into it.

That machine was called Cassini. Its story runs all through tonight's lecture. But first, the planet.

Chapter one. The slow wanderer.

Of all the lights that wander among the stars, Saturn is the slowest.

To the eye, it is a steady, pale, golden point. It does not twinkle. It is not as bright as Jupiter or Venus, but on a clear night, once you know where to look, it is easy to find. And if you watch it from month to month, you will see that it moves... but only just.

It takes Saturn twenty-nine and a half years to travel once around the Sun, and so once around our sky. A person can see it come back to the same stars only two or three times in a whole life.

The people of the ancient world noticed. Saturn was the slowest of the wanderers, and so they placed it farthest away, at the very edge of the heavens. For thousands of years, it was the outermost planet anyone knew.

The Greeks linked it with Kronos, the old god of the age before Zeus... the father of the gods, the one who, in the oldest story, swallowed his own children. The Romans called it Saturn, after their god of the sowing of seed and of the harvest.

In the middle of winter, the Romans held his festival, the Saturnalia. For a few days in December, work stopped, schools closed, people gave each other small gifts and candles, and servants were waited on at table by those they served.

And one day of our week still carries his name. Saturday... Saturn's day.

Saturn is far away.

It is about nine and a half times farther from the Sun than the Earth is... around one and a half billion kilometres. Light from the Sun, which reaches the Earth in a little over eight minutes, takes almost an hour and twenty minutes to reach Saturn.

At that distance, the Sun is a small, bright disk... about one ninetieth as bright as the sunlight on Earth. Still far brighter than the full Moon. But a cold light.

Chapter two. The ears.

In July of the year sixteen-ten, Galileo Galilei pointed his telescope at Saturn.

He had already found four moons going around Jupiter that same year. Now he expected to see a small round disk, like Jupiter's. Instead, he saw something he could not understand. Saturn seemed to have two smaller bodies, one on each side, almost touching it, as if it were three planets pressed together.

His telescope was too small to show what they really were. He described Saturn as having ears.

Galileo did not want anyone else to claim the discovery before he was sure. So he wrote it down as a puzzle... a line of jumbled Latin letters, which only he could put back in order. Unscrambled, it read: I have observed the highest planet to be threefold.

Two years later, he looked again. And the ears were gone.

Saturn was a plain, round disk, with nothing beside it at all.

Galileo was baffled. In a letter, he wondered, half seriously, whether Saturn had swallowed his children, as the god did in the old story.

He was not to know the answer. The ears came back, but in the years that followed they kept changing shape... thick, then thin, then rounded, like handles on a jar. Galileo died without ever learning what he had seen.

The answer came in the sixteen-fifties, from a young Dutchman named Christiaan Huygens.

Huygens and his brother ground their own lenses, better ones, and built a longer telescope. In the spring of sixteen fifty-five, looking at Saturn, he found a moon going around it... the largest one. Today we call it Titan.

And looking at the strange ears, night after night, he worked out what they must be.

He, too, hid his answer in a puzzle of letters. When he finally published it, in a book of sixteen fifty-nine, the puzzle read like this: Saturn is surrounded by a ring, thin and flat, touching it nowhere, and tilted to the path of the planets.

A ring. A flat ring, all around the planet, not touching it anywhere.

And that explained the disappearing ears. Saturn is tilted, like the Earth. As it travels around the Sun over those twenty-nine years, we see the ring from different angles... sometimes from above, wide open, sometimes from below. And twice in every Saturn year, the ring turns exactly edge-on to us.

The ring is so thin that, edge-on, it all but disappears. That is what Galileo saw in sixteen-twelve.

It still happens. The last time was in March of twenty twenty-five. For a few days, seen from the Earth, Saturn lost its rings again, just as it had for Galileo, and then, slowly, they opened once more.

Some twenty years after Huygens, an Italian astronomer working in Paris, Giovanni Domenico Cassini, found something more.

The ring was not one ring. There was a dark line running all the way around it... a gap, separating an outer ring from a brighter inner one. That gap still carries his name. The Cassini Division. It is nearly five thousand kilometres wide... wide enough for the Moon to pass through, with room to spare.

Cassini found four more moons as well. Three centuries later, when people built a spacecraft to go to Saturn, they named it after him.

Chapter three. What the rings are made of.

For two hundred years after Huygens, nobody knew what the rings were made of. Many people imagined them as solid... a great flat hoop of rock or metal, spinning around the planet.

In eighteen fifty-nine, a young Scottish scientist named James Clerk Maxwell showed, with careful mathematics, that this could not be true. A solid ring would be torn apart, or would drift until it struck the planet. A liquid ring would break into waves and pieces.

The only kind of ring that could last, he wrote, was one made of a vast number of separate pieces, each going around Saturn on its own, like a swarm of tiny moons.

He was right. Nearly forty years later, an American astronomer, James Keeler, measured the light from different parts of the rings. He found that the inner parts move faster than the outer parts... exactly as countless separate pieces would, each one in its own orbit.

Today we know what those pieces are.

They are ice. Almost entirely water ice... more than nine-tenths of the rings are frozen water, with a little dust and rock mixed in. That is why they shine so brightly in the sunlight.

The pieces come in every size. Most are like grains of sand, or pebbles, or snowballs. Some are the size of a car. A few are as large as a house. Each one goes around Saturn on its own, nudging its neighbours now and then, in a slow, endless traffic of ice.

The rings are enormously wide. From one outer edge of the main rings to the other is around two hundred and seventy thousand kilometres... about seven tenths of the way from the Earth to the Moon.

And yet in most places they are only about ten metres thick. In some places perhaps a little more. In others, less.

If the rings were made of paper, a single sheet, and the sheet was as thin as the rings are for their size... that sheet of paper would be nearly three kilometres wide.

Astronomers name the rings with letters, in the order they were found... which is why the letters seem to be mixed up.

The two great bright rings are the A ring, on the outside, and the B ring inside it... the brightest and heaviest of all. Between them lies the Cassini Division. Inside the B ring is the dim, faint C ring, which early astronomers called the crepe ring, because it looked like thin, dark gauze. And inside that, closest to the planet, the faintest of all, the D ring.

Outside the bright rings are others, thinner and fainter still. A narrow, twisted strand called the F ring. A thin G ring. And a great, faint, wide one, the E ring, made of fine ice dust, far out among the moons. We will come back to where that dust comes from.

Look closer, and every ring is made of many rings. Hundreds of them, thousands... like the grooves on an old record. Some are sharp and narrow. Some have ripples running through them, like waves spreading on a pond. Some have small gaps where nothing at all goes around.

Most of this pattern is made by moons.

Each moon, as it goes around, pulls very gently on the ring pieces. Where a ring piece goes around exactly twice for every once of a moon, or three times for twice, the small pulls add up, orbit after orbit, and the ice is pushed aside. The Cassini Division itself is cleared that way, by a small moon called Mimas, far outside the rings.

Saturn's rings are shaped, in other words, like sand on a drum. The moons keep the rhythm.

Chapter four. The visitors.

For more than three hundred years after Huygens, the rings could only be seen from the Earth, from very far away.

Then, in September of nineteen seventy-nine, a small spacecraft called Pioneer eleven flew past Saturn. It was the first. It sent back a handful of blurred pictures and many measurements, and it went on its way out of the solar system.

A year later came Voyager one. And nine months after that, its twin, Voyager two.

The Voyagers changed everything. Their cameras showed the rings in detail for the first time... and the rings were far stranger than anyone had guessed.

There were the thousands of narrow ringlets. There was the F ring, which looked braided, as if two or three strands were twisted together. And there were spokes... long, dark, ghostly smudges lying across the B ring, like the spokes of a wheel. They appeared, turned with the ring for a few hours, and faded away. Nobody had ever seen them from the Earth.

And there, at Saturn's north pole, was something even more puzzling. A hexagon.

It is exactly what it sounds like. A great six-sided shape in the clouds, wrapped around the north pole, with straight edges and corners, like a cell in a honeycomb.

Each of its sides is longer than the whole width of the Earth.

It is made by a stream of wind... a jet stream, racing around the pole. Why the stream bends itself into six straight sides, and holds that shape, year after year, is still being studied. Scientists have made similar shapes in the laboratory, in spinning tanks of water, where a fast current running between slower water breaks into a polygon of regular sides.

But on Saturn, it has lasted for at least forty years. And it is wider than two Earths.

The Voyagers flew past and went on, out of the solar system. They are still travelling today. But that is a story for another night.

They had shown that Saturn deserved a longer visit. Not a fly-by of a few days... but someone to stay.

Chapter five. Cassini.

It took many years to plan, build and launch.

The spacecraft was made by NASA, with the European Space Agency and the Italian Space Agency. It was more than six metres tall. Fuelled and ready, it weighed nearly six tonnes... one of the heaviest machines ever sent between the planets. Most of that weight was fuel.

So far from the Sun, solar panels would not have given enough power. Instead, Cassini carried a source of heat... small amounts of plutonium, slowly decaying, warming devices that turned the heat into electricity. It would keep the spacecraft alive for many years.

On its side, Cassini carried a passenger: a smaller probe, shaped like a wide shell, called Huygens. Huygens would leave Cassini at Saturn, and go down to Titan.

Cassini was launched from Florida in October nineteen ninety-seven.

No rocket was powerful enough to send so heavy a machine straight to Saturn. So it took the long way. It flew past Venus, twice. It came back past the Earth. It passed Jupiter. Each time, a planet's pull swung it faster, like a stone from a sling, until it was moving fast enough to reach Saturn.

The journey took nearly seven years.

At the end of June, two thousand and four, Cassini arrived.

To slow down enough to stay, it had to fire its main engine for an hour and a half, and it had to do it while passing through a gap in the rings... between two of the faint outer rings, where there was a little less ice. Saturn was so far away that the signal from the spacecraft took more than an hour to reach the Earth. Nobody could steer it. It had been told what to do, and it did it alone.

When the signal came, it said that Cassini was in orbit. It was the first spacecraft ever to go around Saturn.

It had been meant to stay for four years. It stayed for thirteen.

In all that time, it went around Saturn nearly three hundred times. It flew close past Titan more than a hundred times, using that moon's pull to steer itself from one orbit to the next... sometimes close to the rings, sometimes high above them, sometimes far out among the moons. It saw Saturn's northern winter turn to spring, and spring to summer.

And for thirteen years, its pictures came home. Hundreds of thousands of them.

Chapter six. Titan.

On Christmas Day, two thousand and four, Cassini let go of Huygens.

The little probe drifted away on its own, toward Titan, for three weeks. On the fourteenth of January, two thousand and five, it reached the top of Titan's air, and began to fall.

Titan is the largest of Saturn's moons. It is larger than the planet Mercury. And it is the only moon in the whole solar system with a thick atmosphere... thicker, in fact, than the Earth's.

Because of that air, nobody had ever seen its surface. From outside, Titan looks like a smooth orange ball of haze. Even the Voyagers saw nothing underneath.

Huygens fell through that haze for more than two hours.

It opened its parachutes. It took pictures as it fell. Slowly, out of the orange fog, shapes appeared. Hills. Dark plains. And what looked like river channels, branching like the veins of a leaf, running down from the hills to the lowlands.

Then it landed.

It came down with a soft bump, on ground the consistency of damp sand, or of snow with a crust. It was the first landing ever on a world in the outer solar system... and still, today, the farthest from the Earth that any spacecraft has ever landed.

Huygens' one picture from the ground shows a plain, under an orange sky, scattered with small round stones, the size of pebbles and fists.

The stones were rounded the way river stones are rounded. Worn by flowing liquid.

But they were not rock. On Titan, at almost one hundred and eighty degrees below zero, water is frozen as hard as stone. The pebbles were water ice. And the liquid that had rolled them smooth was not water at all.

The probe kept sending its signal from the surface for more than an hour, until Cassini, which was relaying its voice, passed below Titan's horizon. Huygens may have gone on talking for a while after that, with no one left to listen.

The rivers were real.

Over the next years, Cassini's radar looked through the haze, pass after pass, and mapped the surface. Near the north pole it found lakes, and seas... great dark, smooth stretches, some larger than any lake in North America.

They are filled with liquid methane and ethane. On the Earth, those are gases, the kind that burn in a kitchen stove. On Titan, it is so cold that they are liquid.

Titan has weather. It has clouds of methane, and it rains methane... slowly, in large drops, falling gently through the thick air. The rain runs down the hills into rivers, the rivers into lakes. The lakes give back some of their methane to the air, and the clouds form again.

Rain, rivers, seas, clouds. The same cycle as on the Earth, but with a different liquid, at a different cold. It is the only other place we know of where liquid lies on the surface in seas.

There are dunes, too, near the equator... long dark ridges hundreds of kilometres long. Not of sand, but of grains of frozen organic dust that have fallen out of the sky.

The story of Titan is not finished. A new spacecraft, called Dragonfly, is being prepared for it. It is not a lander that stays in one place. It is a flying machine, with eight rotors, like a large drone, that will hop from place to place through the thick air and low gravity.

If all goes to plan, it will leave the Earth at the end of this decade and reach Titan in the middle of the next.

Chapter seven. The fountains of Enceladus.

Of all the things Cassini found, the most surprising came from a small moon.

Enceladus is only about five hundred kilometres across... small enough to fit inside Great Britain. It is the whitest object in the solar system. Its surface is fresh, clean ice, reflecting almost all the sunlight that falls on it.

For a moon so small, it should be frozen solid, dead and still all the way through.

In two thousand and five, Cassini flew close past it, and found that it was not.

Near its south pole, the surface is cut by four long, parallel cracks, a little warmer than the ice around them. Scientists called them the tiger stripes. And from these cracks, out into space, jets of water vapour and ice grains are spraying... more than a hundred of them. Fountains, rising hundreds of kilometres above the little moon.

When Cassini looked back at Enceladus with the Sun behind it, the fountains lit up, like fine spray against a low sun.

Most of the ice falls back as snow on the moon's surface. Some of it escapes. It drifts into orbit around Saturn, and spreads out into a vast, faint ring... the E ring. The ring of fine ice dust we passed earlier.

So the E ring is made, all the time, by a small moon breathing out.

Cassini did something no one had planned. It flew straight through the fountains, again and again, and tasted them.

It found water. It found salt, like the salt of a sea. It found grains of silica, which on the Earth form where hot water flows over rock. It found simple carbon compounds, and hydrogen gas, and later, in its records, phosphorus... one of the things living cells need.

Beneath the ice of Enceladus, it turned out, is an ocean. A salty ocean covering the whole moon, under a shell of ice many kilometres thick, kept liquid by the heat of Saturn's tides, squeezing and kneading the little moon as it goes around. And at the bottom of that ocean, where water meets rock, there may be warm springs.

Nobody knows whether anything lives there. It may be empty. But it has water, warmth, salt, and the chemicals of life, all in one place... and it is spraying samples of itself into space for anyone who passes by.

For that reason alone, Enceladus has become one of the places people most want to go back to.

Chapter eight. A family of moons.

Saturn has more moons than any other planet.

At the last count, in the summer of twenty twenty-six, the number confirmed was two hundred and ninety-three. Most of them are tiny... rocks a few kilometres across, caught long ago, going around far out, many of them backwards. New ones are still being found.

A handful are large enough to be round. And each of those has its own character.

Mimas, the small moon that clears the Cassini Division, has one enormous crater on its face... so large for its size that the impact which made it must have nearly broken the moon apart. With the crater on one side, Mimas looks a little like a great round eye, watching.

Tethys and Dione are grey-white worlds of ice, covered in craters and long cliffs.

Rhea is larger, old and battered.

And far out is Iapetus... the strangest of them all.

Giovanni Cassini found Iapetus in sixteen seventy-one, and he noticed something odd at once. When the moon was on one side of Saturn, he could see it easily. When it was on the other side, it seemed to vanish.

He guessed, correctly, that one half of Iapetus must be bright, and the other half dark... and that the moon always keeps the same face toward Saturn, so that we see its bright side in one part of its orbit and its dark side in the other.

He was right. One half of Iapetus is as dark as coal. The other half is as white as snow. And around its middle, Cassini the spacecraft found a long, straight mountain ridge running along the equator, like the seam on a walnut. Nobody is sure how it got there.

Then there are the shepherds. Two small moons, called Prometheus and Pandora, go around on either side of the narrow F ring, inside and outside it. Their pull keeps its ice from spreading. Prometheus, each time it comes close, draws long streamers out of the ring, like fingers trailed through water.

There is Pan, a little moon living inside a gap in the A ring, and keeping that gap open. Seen from the side, Pan has a thin ridge of ring dust around its middle... so it looks rather like a walnut, or a small flying saucer.

And there is Daphnis, smaller still, in another narrow gap. As it goes around, its pull raises waves along the edges of the gap... waves of ice, a kilometre or more high, rising and falling as it passes, casting long shadows across the ring.

And Hyperion. It is not round at all, but lumpy, light and full of holes, like a sponge, or a piece of pumice. It is so porous that a large part of it is simply empty space. It does not spin steadily, like most moons. It tumbles... so that nobody can say, from one day to the next, which way it will be facing.

Chapter nine. The planet itself.

So far we have spoken of the rings and the moons. But what of Saturn itself?

It is a giant of gas, like Jupiter. Almost all of it is hydrogen and helium. There is no solid surface. Under the clouds, the air simply grows thicker and hotter, until, deep down, it becomes a liquid, and deeper still, under enormous pressure, the hydrogen behaves like a metal. At the very centre, there is probably a large, fuzzy core of rock and ice, without a clear edge.

Saturn's colours are softer than Jupiter's. Its bands are there, but faint, pale gold and cream and butterscotch, as if seen through a veil. And they are seen through a veil... a high haze, lying over the deeper clouds, softening everything beneath.

Under that calm face, the winds are fierce. At the equator, they blow at well over a thousand kilometres an hour... faster than the fastest winds on Jupiter.

A day on Saturn is short... about ten and a half hours. For a long time, nobody knew exactly how long. Jupiter's day could be timed by its magnetic field, which wobbles as it turns. Saturn's field is almost perfectly lined up with its spin, and gave no clear clue.

The answer, in the end, came from the rings. Deep inside Saturn, the planet very slightly rings like a bell. Its vibrations pull, faintly, on the ring pieces, and leave tiny patterns of waves in the C ring. By measuring those waves in Cassini's data, scientists could feel the planet turning inside. A day on Saturn, they found, is ten hours, thirty-three minutes, and about thirty-eight seconds.

So the rings are not only beautiful. They are an instrument, listening to the planet.

Because it spins so fast, and is so light, Saturn is the most flattened planet in the solar system. It is about a tenth wider across the equator than from pole to pole. Even through a small telescope, it looks a little squashed.

And it is light. Saturn is the only planet in the solar system that is less dense than water... about seven-tenths as dense. If you could find that ocean large enough, it would float.

It would not float for long, of course. It would leave a ring.

About once every thirty years... once in each Saturn year... a great white storm breaks out in its northern half, as the summer comes on. The storm rises from deep below, spreads around the planet in a band of bright clouds, and, over months, fades again.

Astronomers saw them in eighteen seventy-six, nineteen-oh-three, nineteen thirty-three, nineteen-sixty, nineteen-ninety. Cassini was there for the next one, which began in December of twenty-ten, a little early. It grew until its tail wrapped all the way around the planet, and it crackled with lightning for months.

Chapter ten. How old are the rings?

Here is a question that scientists still argue about, and they argue with some feeling.

Have the rings always been there, since the planets formed, four and a half billion years ago? Or are they young... younger than the dinosaurs?

The rings are bright. Very bright, very clean ice. And all the time, the solar system is sprinkling them with dark dust from space. Over billions of years, you might expect them to grow grey, like snow beside a road at the end of winter.

In its last months, Cassini measured how much the rings weigh, and how fast the dust falls on them. Many scientists concluded that the rings are young... perhaps only a few hundred million years old, perhaps less. If that is so, then for most of its history Saturn had no great rings at all. They might have formed when a moon, or a passing body, came too close to the planet and was torn apart.

Other scientists have replied that the rings could be old after all... that dust striking them at such high speed might mostly vaporise and blow away, leaving the ice clean.

The question is not settled. And it may be one of those questions that is not settled for a long time.

There is something else Cassini found. The rings are slowly raining into the planet.

Small grains of ice, charged by sunlight, are drawn along the lines of Saturn's magnetic field, and fall into its upper air. Other material falls from the inner edge of the rings straight down toward the clouds. Little by little, over hundreds of millions of years, the rings may be emptying themselves into Saturn.

So it may be that we happen to live in the age of Saturn's rings. That they came late, and will not stay forever. And that we were lucky enough to arrive while they are here.

Chapter eleven. The day the Earth smiled.

In July of twenty thirteen, Cassini turned its camera back toward home.

It was in Saturn's shadow, with the Sun hidden behind the planet. From there, it could look toward the inner solar system without being blinded. The rings glowed, lit from behind. And just below them, among the stars, there was one tiny blue point of light.

The Earth. More than a billion kilometres away.

Weeks before, the people of the mission had told the world when the picture would be taken. They asked anyone who wished to go outside at that hour, look up toward Saturn, and wave.

Many people did. In their gardens, on rooftops, in parks. They waved toward a planet they could not see, at a spacecraft they could not see, which was taking a picture in which none of them could be seen. They called it the day the Earth smiled.

The Earth in that picture is smaller than a single pixel. The Moon is beside it, fainter still. Everyone who has ever lived is in that point of light.

Chapter twelve. The grand finale.

By the year twenty-seventeen, Cassini was running out of fuel.

It could not be left to drift. If it did, then one day, years later, out of control, it might strike Titan or Enceladus... and carry Earthly germs onto a world that might hold life of its own. The scientists would not take that risk. So they decided that Cassini, when its fuel was nearly gone, would end its life inside Saturn itself.

But first, it would do something no spacecraft had ever done.

Between the innermost ring and the top of Saturn's clouds, there is a gap. About two thousand kilometres wide. Nothing had ever flown through it. Nobody even knew for certain whether it was empty, or full of small grains of ice that might destroy a spacecraft at that speed.

In April, twenty-seventeen, a last pass by Titan bent Cassini's orbit inward. And it dived through the gap.

On the first dive, the spacecraft turned its large dish antenna forward, like an umbrella, to shield itself from whatever might be there. It went through at more than one hundred thousand kilometres an hour. And then it was silent for nearly a whole day, while it gathered its measurements, and turned back toward the Earth.

When it called home, it was safe. The gap was almost empty.

It made twenty-two of those dives, once every week, through the spring and summer of that year.

On each one, it passed closer to Saturn than any spacecraft ever had. It weighed the rings. It measured the planet's gravity and magnetic field from close up, feeling for the core. It flew through the edge of the ring rain, and tasted the material falling from the rings into the clouds. Its cameras looked down on the cloud tops, and back up at the rings, edge-on and close, the ice pieces casting tiny shadows.

In September, a last distant pass by Titan... it was called the goodbye kiss... set Cassini on its final course. It would not come out again.

On the morning of the fifteenth of September, twenty-seventeen, Cassini entered Saturn's atmosphere.

It did not switch its instruments off. To the very end, it kept its antenna pointed at the Earth, and sent back what it was tasting of Saturn's air, as it fell deeper into it. It held its course with its little thrusters for as long as they could manage, against the thickening air.

Then the air won. The spacecraft turned, lost the Earth, and fell silent. Within moments, it would have broken apart and burned, like a meteor, high in Saturn's sky.

But the light it sent was still on its way.

Radio waves cross the space between Saturn and the Earth in about eighty-three minutes. So for nearly an hour and a half after Cassini was gone, the people who had built it, flown it and loved it sat in their control room and watched its signal still arriving, as if it were still alive.

The last of it reached a great dish antenna in Australia, near Canberra. Then the line on the screens went flat.

It had spent thirteen years at Saturn, and twenty years in space. Saturn kept it.

Chapter thirteen. The ringed planet.

Seen from the Earth, Saturn is just a pale, steady point of gold, moving slowly among the stars.

But even a small telescope, the kind a child might get for a birthday, will show the rings. It is a moment people remember. For most of us, Saturn has been a picture in a book all our lives. And then, at the eyepiece, there it is... tiny, sharp, and real, floating in the dark, with its ring around it, just as it looks in the pictures.

Many people, the first time, step back from the telescope and look up, as if to check that it is really there.

If you could stand somewhere above the clouds of Saturn, close to the equator, the rings would not look like a ring at all. Seen almost edge-on, from inside, they would be a thin, bright line, rising out of the haze at one horizon, arching up over the whole sky, and coming down to the other. A bridge of light, crossing the heavens.

By night, it would be lit by the far Sun. On the dark side of it, you would see the shadow of the planet itself, laid across the ice. And all around, the moons would rise and set... Titan, small and orange, and white Enceladus, and dark-and-light Iapetus, each in its turn.

Saturn is lighter than water.

It is nearly ten times as wide as the Earth, and it would float. Around it, the rings turn in their thin, flat circle, as wide as the distance to the Moon and as thin as a house is tall... thousands of rings within the rings, kept in time by the moons.

Galileo saw them as ears. Huygens saw a ring. Cassini fell into its sky.

And tonight, far out beyond Jupiter, the rings are turning still.

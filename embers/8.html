The script

On the north bank of a wide grey river, a narrow lane runs down the hill towards the water.

It is steep, and it is short, and the houses on either side lean in towards each other as they rise... each floor jutting out a little further than the one below it, so that at the top, people living on opposite sides could almost reach across and shake hands.

At the top of the lane, in the dark, there is a bakery. Its oven is cooling. The baker and his family are asleep in the rooms above it.

And below, at the bottom of the hill, the river goes on moving, quietly, out towards the sea.

Embers of History. Episode eight... The Great Fire of London.

It is the year 1666, and the first days of September. You are in London... the old City of London, inside its Roman and medieval walls, on the north bank of the Thames.

You are seventeen. You are a waterman's apprentice. Your master is your own father, and his boat is a wherry... a long, narrow, light boat, pointed at both ends, with a pair of oars and a cushioned seat in the stern for the passengers. Every day, from first light, you row people up and down the river, and across it, from one set of stone stairs to another. There are a few coaches for hire in the streets, slow and dear, and only one bridge. The river is the main road.

You know the city best from the water. Its wharves and its warehouses, its church towers, and the long, crowded bridge with houses built all along it.

How do we know what the next few days were like? Better than for almost any other disaster of its time. Because two men in London, that week, were keeping diaries. One was Samuel Pepys, an official of the Navy Office, who wrote nearly every night, in a private shorthand, for nearly ten years. The other was John Evelyn, a gentleman and a writer, who knew the King. Both of them watched the fire. Both of them wrote down what they saw.

So a great deal of what follows is certain. Some of it is likely. And a few things are only what people said at the time, or have said since. Where it is only a story, I will tell you so.

Chapter one. A city of wood.

London, in 1666, is one of the largest cities in Europe. Inside the old walls, and in the streets that have spread outside them, live several hundred thousand people. Nobody counted them exactly. Nobody yet thought of doing so.

Most of the houses are built of timber. Oak frames, with the spaces between filled in with laths and plaster, and the outside often coated with pitch to keep the weather out. Many are four or five floors high. The upper floors stick out over the street, one above another, so that the lanes below are narrow and dim, and in some places the sky is only a thin strip between the roofs.

Down by the river, near the bridge, are the warehouses... and what they hold is the trade of the whole city. Timber. Coal. Oil. Tallow for candles. Hemp, and pitch, and tar for the ships. Brandy. Barrels of every kind, stacked high.

The summer of 1666 has been long, and hot, and dry. It has hardly rained for weeks. The wooden houses have dried out in the sun, the way kindling dries on a hearth.

And for the past few days, a strong wind has been blowing... from the east, steady and warm, down the river and across the roofs of the city.

The city has had a hard two years. In 1665, the plague came, and through that summer and autumn, the carts went round the streets at night, and many tens of thousands of Londoners died. Those who could afford it left. The King and his court left. Now, a year later, the sickness has faded. People have come back. The shops are open again.

And England is at war with the Dutch, at sea. Only a few weeks ago, an English fleet set fire to a great many Dutch merchant ships, and to a Dutch town. In London, people wonder, quietly, whether the Dutch will want to answer in kind.

Chapter two. Pudding Lane.

The lane that runs down the hill is Pudding Lane. Some say it takes its name from the butchers of Eastcheap, at the top of the hill, who carried their offcuts down it to the river... in the old English of the time, "puddings". But that is only one explanation among several.

Near the top of the lane, in a small yard, stands the bakery of Thomas Farriner.

He is a baker of some standing. He supplies bread to the navy, and he is remembered as a baker to the King. He is a widower, and he lives above the shop with his son, his daughter Hanna, and a servant or two.

On the evening of Saturday the first of September, the bread is baked. The fire in the oven is let down for the night. Farriner goes up to bed. Later, he will swear, again and again, that he raked out the oven properly, and that there was no fire left in the bakehouse when he went upstairs.

Somewhere after midnight, smoke begins to rise through the house.

It is about one o'clock in the morning, on Sunday the second of September, when the household wakes. By then, the stairs are already full of smoke and heat. The family cannot get down to the street. So they climb out of an upstairs window, and along the gutters and roofs, to a neighbour's house... and from there, down to safety.

It is said that one person in the house, a maidservant, did not follow them... and that she was the fire's first loss. Her name, if she had one in the records, has not come down to us.

In the lane, the neighbours come out in their nightclothes. Someone runs for the parish constables. The constables come, and they look at the burning house, and at the houses pressed against it on every side. The only way to stop a fire in a city like this is to pull down the houses in its path, before it gets there, and leave it nothing to eat. But pulling down a house is a serious matter. Whoever owns it will want to be paid. And so the constables send for the one man in the City who can give that order.

He is the Lord Mayor of London, Sir Thomas Bludworth.

He arrives in the small hours, roused from his bed, and he is not pleased to have been. He looks at the fire. He hears about the cost of pulling down the houses, and whose houses they are. And he decides that it is not so very much... and, it is said, he made a coarse remark: that it was small enough for one person to put out alone. Then he goes home to bed.

Whether he really said those words, we cannot be quite sure. They were reported later, and they were repeated, and they have been attached to his name ever since.

Chapter three. Morning on the river.

Before dawn, you are at the stairs, with your father, getting the boat ready. And you can see it already, from the water... a red light above the roofs, near the bridge. The east wind is carrying sparks over the houses, westward, and down the hill towards the river and the warehouses of Thames Street.

A little to the east, in Seething Lane, near the Tower of London, is the Navy Office, where Samuel Pepys lives and works. He is thirty-three years old.

That night, his maids have stayed up late, getting the house ready for a dinner the next day. At about three in the morning, one of them, Jane, comes and wakes him and his wife, to tell them that there is a great fire in the City. Pepys gets up, in his nightgown, and goes to her window, and looks. The fire seems some way off. He has seen fires before. He goes back to bed.

When he gets up again, at about seven, Jane tells him that she has heard that some three hundred houses have burned in the night... and that the fire is still burning, down by the bridge.

So he dresses, and he walks to the Tower, and climbs up onto one of its high places to look. And from there he can see it all: the houses at the end of the bridge on fire, and a great fire spreading along the riverside, beyond.

He goes down to the water, takes a boat, and is rowed upstream, under the bridge, to see more closely.

What he saw on the river that morning, he wrote down that night.

Everywhere along the bank, people are trying to save their goods. They throw them into the river, or drag them into boats and barges. Others stay in their houses until the very last moment, until the fire is touching them, and then run down to the water stairs, and get into a boat, and push off.

And the pigeons. He noticed the pigeons. The city is full of them, nesting in the eaves and on the balconies, and they will not leave their houses. They circle round the windows, round and round, close to the flames... until some of them fly too near.

He noticed one more thing, a small thing, and wrote it down. In the boats on the river, among all the furniture and bedding and boxes that people are saving, there is one kind of thing he keeps on seeing. A pair of virginals... a small keyboard instrument, in a wooden case, the kind a family keeps in its best room. In nearly one boat out of every three, he wrote, there is a pair of virginals.

Out on the water, you row a family of four, and a chest, and a rolled-up feather bed, across to the south bank. Nobody speaks very much. The children watch the smoke. The father watches the house he has left.

On the far side, at the stairs, they climb out, and you hand up the chest, and the feather bed. And then you turn the boat round, and go back for the next.

Chapter four. Whitehall.

It is Sunday morning, and all over the city, the church bells are ringing. Some of them are ringing for the morning service, as on any Sunday. Some, in the parishes near the fire, have begun to ring backwards, from the lowest bell to the highest... the old signal that something is wrong.

Pepys has seen enough. Nobody, he thinks, is doing anything to stop the fire. So he has himself rowed upriver, past the bend in the river, to Whitehall... the great rambling palace where King Charles the Second lives.

And there, in the King's own rooms, he tells the King and his brother, James, the Duke of York, what he has seen. That unless they command houses to be pulled down, nothing can stop the fire.

The King takes it seriously. He tells Pepys to go to the Lord Mayor, at once, and tell him, from the King, to spare no houses... to pull them down in front of the fire, whichever way it goes. And the Duke of York sends a message that, if the Lord Mayor needs soldiers, he shall have them.

Pepys goes back into the City, by coach, and then on foot, through streets filling with people and their belongings, looking for the Lord Mayor.

He finds him at last, in a street near the fire, with a handkerchief tied around his neck, looking like a man who has been up all night. Which he has.

Pepys gives him the King's message. And the Lord Mayor, in Pepys's account, answers almost like a man in despair. What can he do? He is worn out. The people will not obey him. He has been pulling houses down, but the fire is coming on faster than they can do it. He says he needs no soldiers. And he says that he must go home now, for he has been up all night.

And he goes.

In the streets, the fire is spreading westward and northward, house by house, along the river and up the hill. The water that would put it out is close at hand, in the Thames... but at the river end, the fire has already reached the great wooden waterwheels under the bridge, that pumped water up into the pipes of the City. And they are burning.

The city has its own tools against fire. In the churches, along the walls, hang leather buckets, and long poles with iron hooks at the end, for pulling down burning thatch and timber. There are hand squirts, like great brass syringes, and in a few parishes, a fire engine... a wooden tank on wheels with a pump. They are kept for a fire in one house, or two, or three.

They were never meant for this.

Chapter five. The arch.

By evening, the fire has been burning for most of a day. You and your father have been on the river since before dawn. Your arms ache. The light on the water is not the light of sunset. It is coming from the wrong direction.

Pepys and his wife, that evening, take a boat across the river, to the south bank, to a little alehouse on Bankside. And there, in the dusk, they sit and watch.

As it grew dark, he wrote, the fire became more and more visible. It was no longer a fire in some houses. It ran along the riverside, and up the hill, in the corners and the church steeples and between the houses... and as far as they could see, it made one long arch of flame, from one end to the other, more than a mile long.

He could hear it. The cracking of the houses, as they gave way.

It made him weep, he wrote, to see it.

John Evelyn, too, crossed to Bankside, the next day, with his wife and son, to look. He wrote of the light of it. The whole sky above the city, he said, looked like the top of a burning oven. And the light could be seen for many miles around... he heard, for forty miles... for several nights together.

In the city behind the fire, people do not go to bed that night. They are packing.

All that day, out on the water, Pepys had felt the wind. It is still blowing hard, from the east, carrying drops of fire across the river. Pepys and his wife go home by water, and, at home, begin to pack too. It is a bright night, with the moon up, and dry.

Chapter six. The carts.

On Monday, the third of September, before four in the morning, a cart comes to Pepys's door in Seething Lane. A friend has sent it. Pepys loads it with his money, his plate... the silver and pewter of a well-off household... and his best things, and rides with them, still in his nightgown, out through the city to a friend's house at Bethnal Green, in the fields to the east.

All over London, everyone who can find a cart is doing the same.

The streets are full. Carts and horses, porters bent under bundles, people carrying chairs on their heads, children holding lamps and pots and birdcages. The gates of the City are crowded with them, going out into the fields. The price of a cart, that week, climbs and climbs, until only the rich can pay it.

And on the river, you are rowing from first light to last. Boats are going down the river, and across it, low in the water, piled with furniture.

That day, the fire reaches the edge of the business heart of the city. It burns along Thames Street, and up the hill. It burns into Cornhill, and by evening the Royal Exchange, where the merchants meet, is burning too.

On this day, too, there is a new fear in the streets.

England is at war with the Dutch, and with the French. And the rumour goes round, as rumours do, that the fire did not begin by itself... that the foreigners started it. And in several places, people who sound foreign, or who look foreign, are pulled about in the streets, and beaten, by frightened crowds. A Frenchman, it is said, was attacked because the chest he carried was thought to hold fireballs. It held tennis balls.

So the King gives the Duke of York the charge of the city. And James rides through the streets, with his guards, from one place to the next, through those four days. He sets up posts round the edge of the fire... each with soldiers, and with gentlemen of the court, and with constables, and men paid to work... and orders that anyone found in danger from the crowds is to be taken into safety.

It is the Duke, more than anyone, who will fight the fire from now on.

Chapter seven. The cheese and the wine.

Tuesday, the fourth of September. The wind is as strong as ever, and the fire is now very near the Navy Office.

Pepys's neighbour and colleague at the office is Sir William Penn, an admiral. Pepys does not always like him. But on Tuesday, they are working side by side.

Another of their neighbours, Sir William Batten, has already dug a pit in his garden, to bury his wine in. Pepys, that morning, puts some of his office papers into it too.

Then in the evening, Pepys and Penn dig another pit, in the garden, and in it Pepys puts his wine... and his parmesan cheese... and a few other things that he cannot carry away, and does not want to lose.

Why the cheese? A whole Parmesan, brought from Italy, was a valuable thing in London in 1666, a gift for someone important... and Pepys was proud of his. In the middle of the greatest fire his city had ever seen, it was one of the things he chose to bury.

We know that, later, he went back for the wine. About the cheese, the diary says nothing more that anyone has found. Some people like to imagine it is still there, somewhere under Seething Lane. It is more likely that he dug it up, and ate it, and did not think to mention it.

That same evening, Pepys and Penn have a practical idea. The fire is being fought by tired townsmen, and by people trying to save their own houses. But down the river, at the King's dockyards at Deptford and Woolwich, there are hundreds of strong, skilled men... shipwrights and labourers, used to timber, and to heavy work. Pepys writes to the Duke's secretary, asking that the dockyard men be sent up to pull houses down.

They are sent for. And in the next days, they help.

Chapter eight. St Paul's.

Up on the hill in the west of the City stands the cathedral of St Paul's.

It is old. It is nearly six hundred years old, and enormous... one of the longest churches in all Europe. Its tall spire burned, after a lightning strike, a hundred years before, and was never rebuilt. And the great building has been falling into poor repair. That very year, a plan had been agreed to restore it. And so, in that dry September, some parts of the old cathedral are wrapped in wooden scaffolding.

All around St Paul's, in Paternoster Row and the streets beside it, live the booksellers and printers and stationers of London. They are the book trade of the whole country.

Under the cathedral, in its crypt, there is a church within a church, the parish church of St Faith. It is the booksellers' own church. Its walls are thick stone, and it is below the ground. And so, as the fire comes closer, the booksellers carry their stock down into it... cartload after cartload of books, and paper, and the sheets of books still being printed. Nothing, they think, could be safer.

On the evening of Tuesday the fourth, the wind carries burning flakes from the streets below up onto the hill. They land in the scaffolding. The scaffolding burns. And from the scaffolding, the old timber roof catches.

The roof of St Paul's is covered in lead... many acres of it, laid in sheets. In the heat, the lead melts.

John Evelyn saw it. And he wrote, in a few words, what it was like. The stones of St Paul's flew like grenades, he said, and the lead ran melting down the streets in a stream. And the pavements glowed so red that neither man nor horse could walk on them.

The roof falls in. The burning beams and the falling stone break through the floor into the crypt below, into St Faith's, where the books are. And the books burn... for days, people said, a slow fire under the ruins.

That night, the fire also takes the Guildhall, the City's own hall. And it runs on, westward, down the hill, and out through Ludgate, and across the Fleet river, into Fleet Street.

From your boat on the dark river, you can see the cathedral on its hill, outlined against the light. And then, slowly, you cannot.

Chapter nine. The firebreaks.

There is only one way to stop a fire like this. You must take away what it feeds on. Pull down the houses in front of it, clear the timber, and leave it an empty space it cannot cross.

But the fire has been moving faster than the men with their hooks and ropes. A house takes a long time to pull down by hand. And by the time a gap has been made, the wind has often carried the sparks across it.

So, from Tuesday, they begin to use gunpowder.

In the streets, where the fire is coming, they bring barrels of powder into the houses, and blow them down... not to destroy, but to make a gap quickly, wider than the wind can jump.

At the eastern edge of the fire stands the Tower of London... and inside the Tower is the great store of the army's and the navy's gunpowder. If the fire reaches the Tower, people fear, the explosion will be beyond anything the city has ever known.

So the men of the Tower's garrison blow up the houses that stand around it. And the fire, coming east, finds a wide empty space where those houses were. And it stops.

The Tower is saved.

And the King himself is in the streets.

Charles and his brother James are seen, during those days, among the people fighting the fire... on foot, and on horseback, giving orders, giving out money to the workmen, and, more than one witness said, working themselves: passing buckets of water from hand to hand, in the lines, standing in the water and the dirt with everyone else, until they were black with smoke.

Whatever else is said of Charles the Second, his people remembered that.

Then, on the Tuesday night, and through Wednesday, the wind begins to change. It drops. It turns. The great east wind, that has been driving the fire for three days, at last grows quieter.

And now the firebreaks begin to hold. One by one, at the edges, the flames come to an empty space, and find nothing on the other side, and fall back.

By the morning of Thursday, the sixth of September, the fire is out, or almost. There will still be small flames in the ruins, and smoke, and heat in the cellars, for many days. But the Great Fire is over.

Chapter ten. Warm ground.

At two o'clock on Wednesday morning, Pepys's wife woke him with bad news. The fire had reached the church at the bottom of their street, All Hallows by the Tower, which Londoners called Barking church. So he took her, and his clerk, and his gold... more than two thousand pounds of it... by boat down the river to Woolwich, for safety.

He came back early in the morning, expecting to find his house burned. It was not. The fire had been stopped at the porch of Barking church.

So he climbed up the church's tower. And he looked out at what was left.

He called it the saddest sight of desolation that he ever saw. Everywhere, fires still burning, cellars full of fire, and the city flattened... the streets lined with fallen walls, and nothing standing but the towers of the churches, and chimneys, here and there, and the walls of stone.

Then he walked into the City. And he wrote that the ground was so hot, among the coals, that it nearly burned his feet.

He walked along streets he had known all his life, and could hardly find them. And near the Royal Exchange, in its ruins, he saw something he wrote down. A cat, being lifted out of a hole in a chimney, by the wall... its fur singed away by the heat... and yet alive.

Evelyn walked through the City too, on the Friday after. He started at Whitehall, and walked by Fleet Street, and up Ludgate Hill, past St Paul's, and down Cheapside, and round by Bishopsgate, and Aldersgate, and out to Moorfields.

He walked, he said, over heaps of rubbish still smoking, and often did not know where he was. He went into St Paul's. Its roof was gone, and the lead had melted from it, and the great stones had cracked in the heat and split... and yet, among the ruins, the inscription over the great west porch, naming who had built it, was still there, with not one letter spoiled.

And all over the city, on the ground, there is a strange, quiet kind of weather. Smoke rising slowly from the cellars. White ash drifting. And here and there, people coming back, and standing where their houses were, looking for the line of their own walls.

The river is quiet now. Fewer boats. You row your father across it, in the first grey morning that has not been red, and neither of you says anything for a long time. Behind you, on the north bank, the city you have known all your life is a long grey field, with church towers standing in it like the masts of ships.

Chapter eleven. Moorfields.

North of the City wall, beyond the gate called Moorgate, there is a stretch of open ground called Moorfields. It is a public field... a place for walking, for drying washing, for archery practice and games. In the dry summer, the ground is hard and bare.

This is where the people go.

By the end of the week, tens of thousands of Londoners are camped in the fields around the city. At Moorfields, at Islington, at Highgate, along the roads, in the open. Some have tents. Some have sheets stretched over poles, or huts made of boards. Some have nothing at all, only their bundles, and a little fire, and the sky.

Pepys walked there on the Wednesday, and found it full of people, every family sitting by its own goods, keeping them together.

He was hungry, and he bought a loaf of bread there. It was only a plain penny loaf. He paid twopence for it.

Evelyn, too, came out to Moorfields. And he saw the people there, some in tents, some in poor huts, many without enough to cover themselves... people who had been well off a week before, he said, and now had nothing, waiting quietly to see what would become of them.

The King came out to them as well. On the Thursday, it is recorded, Charles rode out to Moorfields and spoke to the crowds. He told them that the fire had been the work of God... that there was no plot, and no French and no Dutch behind it. He told them that he would look after them.

He ordered bread to be brought into the fields from the country round about, and markets to be set up at the edges of the city. He asked the towns around London to take people in, and let them work at their trades.

And slowly, through the autumn, the camps empty. People find rooms with relatives, or outside the city, or move into what is left. By the time the cold weather comes, the fields of Moorfields are mostly fields again.

Chapter twelve. The watchmaker's story.

When something so large happens, people want a reason that is as large as it is. A dry summer, a strong wind, and a baker's oven do not seem enough.

So the rumours went on. And in the middle of September, a young Frenchman was arrested, out in Essex, on his way to the coast. His name was Robert Hubert. He was a watchmaker's son, from Rouen, in Normandy.

And he confessed.

He said that he had started the fire himself. That he had been paid to do it. At first, he said he had thrown a fireball into a house in Westminster... but the fire had never reached Westminster. Then he said it was a bakery, in Pudding Lane. He said he had thrown a fireball in through a window, on a pole.

There were problems with his story from the start. He seemed, to many people who met him, confused, and unwell. When he was taken to Pudding Lane, and asked to show which house it was, he is said to have pointed out the right place... but by then, everyone in London knew where the fire had begun. And later, it was said that the captain of the ship that brought him to England swore that Hubert had not come ashore until after the fire had started.

Even some of the men who tried him, it is said, did not believe a word of it.

But he would not take it back. And the jury found him guilty. And on the twenty-seventh of October, 1666, Robert Hubert was hanged at Tyburn.

Thomas Farriner, the baker, is said to have been among those who stood against him at the trial. Farriner always said that his oven had been cold.

A committee of Parliament looked into the fire that winter. It heard many stories, of fireballs and strangers and suspicious lights. It found nothing that could be proved. And in the end, the fire was declared to have been an accident... the hand of God, as people said then... a great wind, and the dry weather, and a city built of wood.

Today, most historians agree. The fire began, most probably, from the oven in Farriner's bakery, or near it, in the small hours of a Sunday. And nobody meant it.

Chapter thirteen. How many.

In the four days of the fire, it destroyed about thirteen thousand two hundred houses. Eighty-seven of the City's parish churches. St Paul's. The Guildhall. The Royal Exchange. The halls of most of the City's companies. The prisons. Gates of the City wall. The roads and the lanes, and the markets.

Inside the walls, about four-fifths of the City was gone. And most of its people, tens of thousands of them, had lost their homes.

And yet, in all of that, the number of people written down as killed by the fire is very small. Six, in the usual count. Perhaps a few more.

It is a number that has been repeated for three and a half centuries. And historians, today, do not trust it.

They point out a few things. That the people who were written down, in records like these, were mostly people with a name and a place... someone who would be missed, and looked for. That the poor of the city, and the people living in its alleys, and the old, and the sick, and those with nobody to look for them, were much less likely to be counted.

That the fire burned, in places, hot enough to melt iron and to crack stone... and in such heat, a person can leave very little behind to be found.

And that the fire did not stop doing harm when the flames went out. Through the autumn, people were living in the fields, without houses, without money, and without work. Then winter came. Some of them, surely, did not live through it, and nobody wrote it down as the fire.

So how many people died in the Great Fire? Six is the number on the page. The true number may have been much larger. One writer, in a long modern history of the fire, has argued that it could have been many hundreds, or more. Others think that too high.

Honestly, nobody knows. And it is likely that nobody ever will.

Chapter fourteen. Brick and stone.

Within days of the fire, while the ground was still warm, people began drawing plans for a new London.

Christopher Wren, a young astronomer and mathematician who had recently turned to architecture, was first. Within a week, he brought the King a plan: a city of wide, straight streets, with open squares, and long avenues meeting in great circles, like a city in Italy or France. Two days after him, John Evelyn brought his own plan. Robert Hooke, a scientist of the Royal Society, drew one too.

The King looked at them all. And in the end, none of them was used.

The reason was simple. Every piece of ground in the burned city belonged to somebody. Under the ash, every house had its owner, its walls, its line on the ground. And the people wanted to build on their own land again, and soon. So the new London was built, mostly, on the old streets... a little wider, a little straighter, but the same.

First, the ground had to be measured. Surveyors went out over the ash, with their chains and staffs, and marked out where each house had stood, and wrote it down.

And then there was the question of who should pay. A tenant whose house had burned might still owe rent for it, for years, under his lease. A landlord might not have the money to build again. Who should rebuild, and at whose cost?

So Parliament set up a special court to decide. It was called the Fire Court, and it sat from February 1667, in the hall of Clifford's Inn, just off Fleet Street. Its judges were the King's own judges, and they sat early in the morning, and they did not charge the usual fees.

Landlords and tenants came before them, one pair after another. And the judges listened, and decided, case by case... who should rebuild, who should pay what, how long a lease should run. Quickly. Fairly, most people thought. And they did not let a quarrel stop a house from being built.

That same February, the Rebuilding Act was passed, with new rules for the new city.

There would be no more timber fronts. Every new house was to be built of brick, or of stone. The upper floors could no longer jut out over the street. The walls between houses were to be thick. And the houses were to be built in a few set sizes... two floors in the small lanes, three on the streets of note, four on the high and principal streets.

To pay for the churches and public buildings, a tax was put on the coal that was brought into London by sea.

And so, over the next few years, London rose again, out of its own ash, and in a different colour. Red brick and pale stone, where there had been black timber and white plaster. Wide doorways. Flat fronts. Tall, regular windows.

Most of the houses were built again within about ten years. The bigger buildings took much longer.

Christopher Wren was given the work of rebuilding the City's churches. Not all eighty-seven were rebuilt. Some parishes were joined together. But fifty-one new churches were built to his designs, each one different, each one fitted to a cramped and odd-shaped piece of ground in the old street plan. And each one with its tower, or its spire, rising above the new roofs... in stone, in lead, in wood and gilding... so that the skyline of the City became a forest of steeples.

And above them all, on the hill where the old cathedral had burned, he built a new St Paul's.

The first stone was laid in 1675. It took more than thirty years to build. Wren was in his forties when it began, and nearly eighty when, in 1711, the cathedral was declared to be finished. He lived to see it. On its top, he set a great dome, which still rises above the city today.

Chapter fifteen. The Monument.

At the bottom of the hill, near where Pudding Lane meets the street that runs down to the bridge, the city built one more thing. A column, to remember the fire.

It was designed by Christopher Wren, and by his friend Robert Hooke, and it was built between 1671 and 1677. It is a single great column, in pale Portland stone, fluted from top to bottom, standing on a tall carved base.

It is 202 feet high. And it stands 202 feet from the place in Pudding Lane where the bakery of Thomas Farriner is thought to have been. So that, if the column ever fell over, towards the east, its top would land, more or less, where the fire began.

Inside, a narrow stone staircase winds up through the middle of the column... 311 steps, round and round, to a small balcony near the top. And from the balcony, you can look out over the whole city, and the river.

Above the balcony, at the very top, is a great gilded urn, with flames of gold rising out of it.

Hooke, who was a scientist first of all, hoped to use the column for his experiments, as well as a memorial. Its hollow middle, open to the sky, could serve, he thought, as a kind of enormous telescope tube, pointing straight up. There is a small room underneath it, too, for the instruments. It was not, in the end, very useful for them. London traffic, then as now, kept the ground shaking.

On the base of the column are carved long Latin words about the fire... how it began, how far it spread, how it was stopped, and how the city was rebuilt.

In 1681, in a time of fear and anger, a line was added to them, blaming the fire on a Catholic plot. It was taken off under a Catholic king, and cut again after he fell. And in 1830, it was taken away for good. And today, the words on the stone say only what is known.

The fire is long over, in your September of 1666. The river runs on below the ruined city. The stairs are still there, at the water's edge, and the boats are still tied up at them, rocking a little in the wake of a barge going by.

Your father ties up the wherry, and you climb the stone stairs, and walk the short way home along the waterfront, in the dusk, through the smell of cold ash.

Behind you, out on the river, the tide is turning. It comes up from the sea twice a day, and goes back twice a day, as it has always done, under the bridge, past the city, whatever the city does.

On the north bank of a wide grey river, a narrow lane runs down the hill towards the water.

It is still there. The houses are brick now, and stone, and glass. The lane is a little wider. And at the bottom of the hill, a tall pale column stands, with a golden fire on the top of it, that never goes out... and never burns.

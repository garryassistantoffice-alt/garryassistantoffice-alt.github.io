The script

In a small town by the sea, the walls are covered in writing.

Not carved, and not in gold, the way great cities write on their monuments. Painted, in red and black, in big careful letters a hand-width high. Scratched with a nail into the plaster, small and crooked. Names. Greetings. Prices. Jokes. Notices of houses to let. Messages from one friend to another. Votes for the town's next magistrates, painted only last night... and, here and there, somebody who has simply written that they were here.

The whole town is talking. And for once, nearly two thousand years later, we can still hear it.

Embers of History. Episode seven... Pompeii's last ordinary day.

It is the year seventy-nine after Christ. You are in Italy, in the south, on the wide blue bay that is now called the Bay of Naples... in the town of Pompeii.

You are fifteen. You were born here, in a room above a shop on a side street, and you have never been further away than Nuceria, a morning's walk inland, where your mother's family lives. Your uncle is a baker. For two years now, you have worked for him... at the mill, at the oven, at the counter, and running the bread to the houses that order it. You know every street in the town by its stones.

How do we know what it was like? Better than for almost any other town of the ancient world, and for a sad reason. This town was buried, all at once, and kept. Its streets and houses, its shops and paintings and the writing on its walls, its pots and lamps and even some of its bread... were left under the ground for more than sixteen hundred years. And then, slowly, they were dug up again.

So a great deal of what follows is certain. Some is likely. And a little is only what people have guessed, or what the old stories say. Where it is only a guess, I will tell you so.

There is one more thing we are not quite sure of. The day itself. It is a warm day, late in the summer, or early in the autumn... and before the end of this story, you will hear why nobody can yet say which.

Chapter one. The bakery.

Your day begins in the dark, long before the first light, with the sound of the mill.

The bakery stands in the middle of the town. Today, people call it the bakery of Modestus... though who Modestus was, and whether he ever baked a loaf here, nobody can now be sure.

In the room behind the shop stand the mills, and they are the strangest machines in the town. Each one is made of two pieces of grey volcanic stone. The lower stone is a cone, standing up from a round base, like a loaf of sugar. The upper stone is hollow, shaped like an hourglass, and it fits down over the cone like a cap. Through its narrow waist runs a wooden beam, and a donkey, harnessed to the beam, walks slowly round and round the mill. Grain goes in at the top. Flour comes out at the bottom, dusting the stone base white.

The donkey walks the same circle every morning, round and round, at its own pace.

Inside, the oven is already hot. It is a low dome of brick and stone, with a mouth at the front. A fire of wood has been burning in it for an hour. Now your uncle rakes the ashes out, sweeps the floor of the oven clean, and it is ready.

The loaves are round, and about as wide as two hands side by side. Before they go in, each one is pressed across the top with a stick or a cord, making lines like the spokes of a wheel... eight wedges, so that the bread can be broken into eight pieces without a knife.

Your uncle slides them in, on a long wooden paddle, one after another, until the floor of the oven is covered. Then he closes its mouth.

In 1862, archaeologists opened the oven of this bakery... and found it full. Inside were loaves... about eighty of them... still in their places, turned to charcoal, black and hard, but still round, and still marked with their wedges. You can see some of them today, in the museum in Naples.

Pompeii has some thirty bakeries that we know of, or perhaps more. Most of the town's people buy their bread rather than bake it, because most of them have no oven at home. Many of them have no kitchen at all.

So when the first loaves come out, warm and brown and smelling the way bread has always smelled, there is already a line at the counter.

Chapter two. The street.

Your first round of bread goes out with the sunrise, in a basket on your arm.

You step out of the shop into the street, and you step up. The pavements in Pompeii are high... a step above the road, sometimes two. The road itself is paved with big, many-sided blocks of dark grey lava, fitted together like the pieces of a puzzle, and worn smooth and slightly hollow down the middle.

There are no drains under most of the streets. When it rains, the water runs down the roads themselves, as though they were little rivers. And the fountains on the corners overflow all day long, and the water runs down the streets from them too, washing away what the town drops in them.

So to cross the road without wetting your feet, you use the stepping stones.

They stand in the road at the corners, in a row: big, flat-topped blocks of stone, as high as the pavement. One, two, or three of them, depending on how wide the street is. You cross from pavement to pavement, stone to stone, with the water running quietly between them.

And the carts? They pass between the stones. The gaps are just wide enough for a cart's wheels to roll through on either side, while the mule or the donkey steps over the middle stone.

Over the years, the iron rims of the wheels have cut grooves into the paving, two parallel ruts down the road, worn into the stone itself.

Archaeologists today have studied those grooves very carefully... where they are deep, and which way their edges are worn, on which corners carts have scraped the kerb as they turned. And they think they can tell, from the wear alone, that some of the narrow streets of Pompeii were one way. Traffic in this town, it seems, had rules.

A great house here shows the street almost nothing: blank walls, a few small windows, a doorway. On each side of the doorway there are often shops, open to the street, with wooden shutters taken down in the morning.

As you go by with your basket, the shops are opening, one after another. A man who sells lamps. A man who mends shoes. A woman with a table of eggs and green figs.

It is going to be a fine day.

Chapter three. The water.

At the corner where your street meets a wider one, there is a fountain.

It is a plain, heavy basin made of slabs of stone, and out of a carved head on the slab at the back runs a steady stream of water. It has been running all night. It will run all day. It never stops.

There are some forty fountains like this in the streets of Pompeii, so that nobody in the town has very far to walk for water. Women and children come with their jars. A carter lets his mule drink. You put your basket down on the kerb, cup your hands, and drink too. The water is cold, and very good.

That water has come a very long way.

It began in springs in the mountains, far inland, more than a day's walk from here. From there, the great aqueduct of the bay carries it, through tunnels and along channels, sloping always gently downhill. It was built in the time of the first emperor, Augustus, about a hundred years ago, to bring water to all the towns of the bay. And at Pompeii, a branch of it comes into the town at its highest point, by the gate in the north wall.

There, in a small brick building with a little arched front, the water pours into a basin, and is divided... most scholars think, into three. Three great lead pipes leave the building, and run down into the town, under the pavements.

Along the streets, you can see where those pipes go. Every so often, standing against a wall, there is a tall, narrow pillar of brick and stone, about three times the height of a man. At the top of each one is a tank of lead.

The water climbs up a pipe into the tank, and then falls down another pipe on the far side, and goes on. Each tower takes away a little of the water's force. Without them, coming down the long slope of the town, the water would arrive with so much pressure that the pipes would burst.

From the pipes, smaller pipes run to the fountains, to the public baths, and to the houses of those who can pay for water of their own.

This morning, as you go along with your basket, there are men in the street with picks.

They have lifted the paving stones in a long line, and dug a trench, and there in the trench lies a length of new lead pipe, grey and dull, waiting to be joined. All over the town, in these years, the water is being repaired. Some of it has been broken for a long time.

For in this town, everyone remembers the earthquake.

Chapter four. The earthquake.

It came about seventeen years ago... on a winter's day, on the fifth of February. You were not yet born. But your uncle remembers it, and so does everyone over twenty.

The ground shook. Walls cracked and fell. Statues tumbled. In the country nearby, it is said, a flock of six hundred sheep died where they stood. The great philosopher Seneca, in Rome, wrote about it soon afterwards. He said that Pompeii, a busy town in Campania, had been struck down by an earthquake... and that the town of Herculaneum, along the coast, was partly in ruins, and that even what was left standing there was not safe.

Here is a small puzzle for the scholars. Seneca gives the year of the earthquake one way, and the historian Tacitus gives it another. One points to the year sixty-two, the other to sixty-three. Most scholars think Seneca's copyists made a slip... but a few think there were two earthquakes, one after another, and that both writers are right. Either way, it does not change much for you, or your uncle, or the town.

Because what the earthquake did was simple. It broke Pompeii. And for seventeen years, Pompeii has been mending.

Everywhere you look, there is work going on.

In one house, the painters are back, covering a cracked wall with fresh plaster and fresh colour. In another, a room is full of builder's lime, and a heap of roof tiles. One of the town's temples, the temple of Venus, the town's own goddess, is being rebuilt from the ground. A great new set of public baths, near the middle of the town, is being built on a whole block of its own... and it is still not finished.

And there are the little shakes. Every now and then, the ground trembles... a jar rattles on its shelf, a dog barks, and then everything is still again. People have grown used to it. This is Campania. The ground here has always trembled a little.

Chapter five. The walls that talk.

Your round takes you down the main street, and all along it, you read as you walk.

Not everyone in Pompeii can read. But a great many people can read a little, enough for the walls. And the walls are full.

The biggest letters are the election notices. Every year, the town chooses its magistrates... two men to be in charge, and two more to look after the streets, the markets and the public buildings. And every year, the walls fill with notices, painted in red, in big, elegant capital letters, asking you to vote.

They are short and to the point. One asks you to make Gaius Julius Polybius an aedile, a magistrate of the streets and markets... and then gives you the reason, in three words. He brings good bread.

Your uncle has always said that is the only kind of argument that ever wins an election.

The notices are the work of professional sign painters, with a ladder and a pot of red paint, and some of them, it seems, worked at night, when the streets were quiet. Sometimes they signed their work.

There are more than two thousand of these notices known from the town, and new ones are still found. Only in 2023, archaeologists digging in a house found a whole row of them painted on a wall inside it, asking for votes for one man... a man whose initials, they think, are also carved on a millstone of the bakery in that same house.

Then there are the smaller writings... scratched into the plaster, at the height of a person's hand, with a nail, a stick, a stylus.

They say everything. On one wall, someone has kept a list, day after day, of what was bought... bread, above all, and cheese, and oil, and onions. One person greets another by name, and wishes them well. A shopkeeper writes that a bronze pot has gone missing from the shop, and promises a reward to whoever brings it back. And here and there are the opening words of the poet Virgil's great poem, the one every schoolchild learns.

And on several walls in the town... in the great hall by the forum, at the theatre, and at the amphitheatre... somebody has scratched the same little verse. It speaks to the wall itself. I wonder, wall, it says, that you have not fallen down in ruins... when you hold up so much tiresome writing.

The wall did not fall down. And the writing, tiresome or not, is the reason we know these people's names.

Chapter six. The forum.

By the middle of the morning, the bread is delivered, and your uncle sends you with an empty basket to the forum, to buy salt and oil.

The forum is the heart of the town: a long open square, paved with pale stone, with colonnades of columns down both its long sides, for shade. No carts may come in here. At its entrances, the streets are closed with stones and steps, so the square belongs to people on foot.

All around it stand the buildings that run the town. The great hall, the basilica, with its tall columns, where the law courts sit and the merchants meet. The offices of the magistrates, and the hall where the town council gathers. A market hall for meat and fish, with a round building in its courtyard where, it is thought, the fish were cleaned. And a large building put up by a woman named Eumachia, a public priestess, and one of the richest people in the town... whose statue was set up in it by the fullers of Pompeii.

At the north end of the square, on a high platform, stands the temple of Jupiter, the chief god of Rome.

And behind it... framed between its columns, as though the architects had put it there on purpose... rises the mountain.

It is called Vesuvius. It stands about a morning's walk away, to the north, across the plain: a broad, green, gentle mountain, with vineyards climbing high up its slopes, and woods above them, and fields all around its feet. The wine from its slopes is famous. There is a painting in a house in the town, in its household shrine, of the god of wine himself, dressed in a great bunch of grapes, standing beside a mountain that is most likely this one. It is a single green peak, with vines all over it.

Nobody in Pompeii thinks of the mountain as dangerous. It has always been there, and it has always been quiet. As far as anyone knows, it has never done anything at all.

A Greek geographer, Strabo, who wrote about this coast some years before you were born, did notice something. The very top of Vesuvius, he said, is flat, and barren, and its rocks look the colour of ashes, as though they had been eaten by fire... as though, long ago, there had once been fire there, and it had gone out.

He noticed. Nobody else seems to have paid much attention.

The temple of Jupiter itself is not quite finished being mended. The earthquake cracked it. In a house nearby, a banker named Caecilius Iucundus had a small stone carving set up in his household shrine, showing the temple shaken by the earthquake. A thank-offering, perhaps, for surviving it.

You buy your salt, in a twist of cloth. You buy your oil. And then, because it is nearly noon and you have been working since the middle of the night, you go to find something to eat.

Chapter seven. The counter.

You do not go home to eat. Hardly anyone does, at midday. You go to a counter.

There are counters like this all over Pompeii... eighty of them, perhaps, or more. Today they are often called by a Greek name, thermopolium, which means a place where something warm is sold. They are the town's street kitchens. And for anyone with no kitchen of their own, which is a great many people, they are where dinner comes from.

Each one is a counter of masonry, L-shaped, facing the street, with an opening at the end to slip in and out behind it. Set into the top of the counter, in a row, are big round pots of clay, sunk right down into it, so that only their mouths show. In them are the day's dishes: lentils, beans, a stew, olives, wine. Behind the counter there is a small stove, and shelves of jugs and cups.

The one you like has a painted front. Not long ago, in 2019 and 2020, archaeologists uncovered a counter like this in a corner of the town, with its paintings still bright. A sea-nymph riding a horse of the sea. Two ducks hanging upside down, ready for the pot, and a rooster. A dog on a lead. And in the bottom of the pots, the remains of the last day's cooking... duck, and pork, and goat, and fish, and snails, all in together.

You eat standing up, at the counter, with the street going by behind you. Bread, of course... your uncle's. A bowl of lentils. A handful of olives. And a small cup of wine, well mixed with water.

On the lentils, there is a dash of the sauce that every cook in the bay puts on almost everything. It is called garum. It is made from fish... small fish and the insides of big ones, salted, and left out in the sun, in great vats, for weeks, until they turn to a clear brown liquid. It smells, while it is being made, exactly as you would expect. But a few drops of it make anything taste better.

The leading maker of garum in the town is a man named Umbricius Scaurus. He is very proud of it. In the floor of his house there is a mosaic... of little jars of his own sauce, in black and white stones, each one with a label on it, saying what kind of garum it is, and that it comes from his workshop.

It is not every man who puts his own sauce jars on his floor. But then, his sauce is found in kitchens all over the town.

Chapter eight. The fullers.

After the meal, there is one more errand. Your uncle's good tunic has to be fetched from the fullers.

The fullery is on the main street. Here, cloth is cleaned... new woollen cloth from the loom, washed and thickened, and old clothes too, washed and whitened and made like new.

In the back of the workshop, in a row of small stone tubs, men stand up to their knees in water, treading the cloth. They hold on to the low walls on either side of them and step, and step, and step, as though they were walking up a hill that never ends.

What the water in the tubs has in it is less pleasant. To clean wool, the fullers use a kind of earth, which soaks up grease... and urine, which does the same. It is collected for them, it is thought, in jars set out in the streets.

In Rome, the emperor Vespasian put a tax on the urine collected there. The historian Suetonius says that when his son complained about it, the emperor held up a coin from the first payment, and asked whether the smell offended him. No, said his son. And yet, said the emperor, it comes from urine.

After the treading, the cloth is rinsed, dried, brushed, and pressed in a great wooden press with a screw. Your uncle's tunic comes back to you folded, white and faintly warm from the sun. It smells clean... more or less.

The fullers have a goddess, Minerva, goddess of crafts, and her bird, the owl. And by a doorway in the town, someone has scratched a joke on the first line of Virgil's great poem. Virgil sang of arms and a man. The writer says that he sings of fullers... and an owl.

Chapter nine. The baths.

In the afternoon, when the bread is done and the shop is shut, half the town goes to the baths. And so do you.

The oldest baths in Pompeii are near the middle of the town, and have been here for nearly two hundred years. You go in through a corridor, into a wide courtyard, open to the sky, where young men are running, wrestling, and throwing a ball against a wall.

You leave your clothes on a shelf in the changing room, and go through the rooms, one by one. A warm room first. Then the hot room, where the floor is raised on little pillars of brick, and the heat from a furnace flows underneath it and up through the hollow walls, so that you have to wear wooden sandals so as not to burn your feet. There is no soap. You rub olive oil on your skin, and scrape it off again with a curved metal blade, and the dirt comes off with it.

And then, last of all, the cold room. It is round, with a domed roof, and in the dome there is a single round opening to the sky. A circle of light falls down into a round pool of cold water. You step down into it, and the heat of the hot room is gone, all at once.

The women of the town have baths of their own, in a separate part of the building, with their own entrance. And the new baths, the ones still being built, will be better, everyone says. More light. When they are finished.

Chapter ten. The arena.

On the way home, you walk the long way round, by the amphitheatre.

It stands in the far corner of the town, against the town wall: a great oval bowl of stone and earth, its seats rising in rows all around a sandy floor. On the outside, double staircases climb up to the upper seats. Inside, there is room for about twenty thousand people... more than live in the whole town. On the days of the games, people come from all the towns around.

It is old. It was built about a hundred and fifty years before your time, when Rome sent new settlers here, and the two men who paid for it were very proud of that. It is one of the earliest amphitheatres ever built of stone, anywhere. The great one in Rome, that the people there are only now finishing, is much bigger. But this one came first.

There are no games today. The arena is quiet, and the sand on its floor is raked smooth, and a few children are running up and down the empty rows.

But people here still talk about the riot.

It happened twenty years ago, at a show of gladiators. People had come from Nuceria, your mother's town, to watch. There were insults, as there are between neighbouring towns. Then stones. Then swords. The Pompeians, the historian Tacitus says, had the better of it, and many of the visitors went home hurt.

The emperor sent the matter to the Senate in Rome. And the Senate's answer was stern. No more such shows in Pompeii, for ten years.

Somebody in the town painted the riot on the wall of his house... the amphitheatre seen from above, the crowd fighting on its seats and in the square outside, and a great awning stretched over the top against the sun. It is a strange thing to want on your wall. But perhaps, for a Pompeian, it was a happy memory.

Whether the ban lasted the full ten years, nobody now knows. But on the walls of the town there are painted notices announcing shows: pairs of gladiators, a hunt of wild animals, and awnings, against the sun. One of them, a few years old now, was signed by its painter, a man named Aemilius Celer. He added, underneath, that he had written it alone... by the light of the moon.

You read it on the way home, as you always do.

Chapter eleven. The garden.

In the late afternoon, the light softens and turns gold, and your uncle sends you with a last basket of bread, to one of the great houses near the north of the town.

You knock, and the doorkeeper lets you in. And for a moment, you stand in the doorway and look.

A great house in Pompeii is turned inward, away from the street. You step from the noise and dust of the road into a hall that is cool and dim and quiet. Its roof is open in the middle, a square of sky, and below the opening is a shallow pool of water, to catch the rain. The floor is a mosaic, of tiny stones. The walls are painted, from floor to ceiling, in deep red and black and yellow, with panels of little pictures... a landscape, a god, a bowl of fruit, a bird on a branch.

In one house in the town, the floor of the entrance shows a dog on a chain, black on white, with its teeth bared, and two words under it. Beware of the dog. In another, the stones on the doorstep say, quite simply... welcome, profit.

Beyond the hall, through a wide opening, lies the garden.

It is a square of green, enclosed on every side by a colonnade of painted columns, so that you can walk all around it in the shade. There are low hedges of box, and beds of flowers, roses and lilies and violets. There are little statues on pedestals, and bronze figures that spout water into marble basins. Fruit trees, a fig, a lemon, a pear. Somewhere in the corner, a small fountain is running. And on the back wall, where the real garden ends, a painted one begins: more trees, more flowers, painted birds on painted branches, so that the garden seems to go on further than it does.

The cook takes your bread. The doorkeeper gives you a small coin, which you did not expect. And you go back out into the street, as the first lamps are lit.

Chapter twelve. The evening.

The light goes slowly. In the west, over the sea, the sky turns orange, and then pink, and then a deep, soft blue.

From the walls of the town, high above the plain, you can see a long way. The bay, wide and still, with the little boats coming in. The town of Stabiae, along the shore. Across the water, far off, the cape where the emperor's fleet lies at anchor, at a place called Misenum. And to the north, the mountain, dark green against the evening sky.

The bakery is closed when you get home. Your uncle is sitting in the doorway, on a stool, with a cup of wine, talking to the man who mends shoes.

You eat in the room upstairs. Bread, cheese, a few figs, a little wine. The lamp on the shelf is a small clay lamp, with a spout, and a flame no bigger than a fingertip, feeding on olive oil. It throws long, soft shadows on the plaster.

Outside, the streets go quiet. A late cart rumbles by, its wheels in the old grooves of the road. Somewhere, a door is barred for the night. The water in the fountain at the corner goes on running, as it always does.

And on a wall somewhere in the town, in the dark, by the light of a lantern... or of the moon... a painter is lettering a new notice, in fine red capitals, for the next election.

Tomorrow, the mill will turn again before dawn, and the donkey will walk its circle, and the oven will be lit, and the loaves will go in, with their eight wedges.

That is how it should be. That is how it has been, every day, for as long as you can remember.

Chapter thirteen. What the letters say.

You will never know what happened next. But we can. And it can be told quietly, as history, from a long way off.

Across the bay, at Misenum, the commander of the emperor's fleet was a man named Pliny... a scholar, too, who had written a great book about everything in the natural world. Staying with him was his nephew, a young man of seventeen, also called Pliny.

About twenty-five years later, the historian Tacitus asked the nephew what he remembered of those days. And the nephew wrote him two letters, which have survived. They are the only account we have from someone who was there.

In the early afternoon, he wrote, his mother pointed out to his uncle a cloud of a very unusual size and shape, rising over the far side of the bay. From that distance, nobody could tell which mountain it came from. Its shape, he said, was most like a pine tree... the tall pines of Italy, with a long, straight trunk, and branches spreading out wide at the top.

His uncle, being a scholar, wanted to see it closer. Then a message came, from a woman whose house stood on the shore at the foot of the mountain, asking for help. And so he took the fleet's ships across the bay, to bring away the people living along the coast. He did not come home. His nephew wrote that he died on the shore near Stabiae, quite calmly, it seemed, with his companions near him.

The ash and the stone from the mountain fell on Pompeii for many hours, and then, the next morning, it was covered over completely. Many of its people had already left. Some had not. And the town, its streets, its counters, its walls and their writing, and the loaves in the oven, lay under the ground, deeper than the height of two people.

And the date? Here the scholars have argued for a long time, and quite cheerfully.

The nephew's letter, as it is usually read, gives the day as the ninth day before the first of September... the twenty-fourth of August. And for centuries, everyone took that to be the day.

But the town itself seemed to disagree. People digging there found things that seemed to belong to the autumn. Fruits like chestnuts and pomegranates. Big jars of wine, already sealed, as though the grapes had been picked and pressed. Heavier clothes. And one later Roman historian, writing about the eruption, said simply that it happened in the autumn.

Then, in 2018, in a house that was being dug out for the first time, archaeologists found a few lines of writing on a wall. Not painted, and not scratched... written in charcoal, which smudges and fades very easily, so that it was probably written not very long before. It gives a date, the way Romans wrote them... the sixteenth day before the first of November.

That is the seventeenth of October.

It has no year on it. Somebody may simply have written it a year before, on a wall that was waiting to be painted. And some scholars now argue that the August date may have been right after all. The question is still open... and it may stay open, for a long time.

So we do not know, quite, what day it was. Only that it was an ordinary one.

Chapter fourteen. Found again.

For a very long time, Pompeii was simply gone. Its name was remembered, in the old books. But where it had been, nobody knew. Over its streets lay fields and vineyards, and the mountain stood above them, quiet again.

In the seventeen hundreds, the king of Naples began to dig into the ground along the bay, looking for statues and treasures for his palace. In 1748, his engineer, a Spaniard named Alcubierre, began digging here. He thought he had found Stabiae. He was not sure what he had found.

Then, in 1763, the diggers found a stone with an inscription on it, which named the town council... of the Pompeians. And the town had its name again.

The digging in those first years was not careful. It was a search for beautiful things, and the plain things... the pots, the jars, the walls with their writing... were often broken, or thrown away, or left to fall down.

That began to change in the eighteen sixties, with a scholar from Naples named Giuseppe Fiorelli, who was put in charge of the digging in 1863.

He brought order. He divided the town, on his plan, into nine regions, and each region into blocks, and he gave every doorway in every block a number. So that every house, and every shop, and every counter, has an address of three numbers, which archaeologists still use today. The bakery of Modestus, to them, is region seven, block one, door thirty-six.

He had the town uncovered from the top down, layer by layer, and he wrote down what was found in each layer, and where.

And he had one idea that was entirely his own. Where something made of wood or cloth had been buried in the ash, and had slowly rotted away, it left behind it a hollow, in its own shape. So Fiorelli's workmen poured liquid plaster into the hollows, and let it set, and then gently cleared away the ash around it. And out of the ground came the shapes of things long gone... a wooden door, the shutters of a shop, pieces of furniture. Later, the same method, used in the gardens, brought back the roots of the trees.

Today, the town is open to the sky again.

More than four million people come every year to walk through it. They come in through the gates, and walk down the same streets, on the same lava paving, between the same high kerbs. They cross the roads on the stepping stones, between the grooves cut by the wheels. They stand in the forum, and look past the temple of Jupiter at the mountain, which is grey and bare now, and a different shape, and quiet.

They see the mills of the bakery, standing where they stood. They read the red letters of the old election notices, still on the walls.

And the town is not finished being found. About a third of it still lies under the ground, unexcavated. Every few years, a new house comes up into the light... a new painting, a new counter, a few new words on a wall. The charcoal date was found in 2018. The bright counter with its ducks, in 2020. The election notices in the house by the bakery, in 2023.

The streets are dark now, in the year seventy-nine. Your uncle has barred the shop door. Up in the room above, the little lamp is out. The donkey stands in its stall, beside the quiet mill. The oven is cooling.

And at the corner, the fountain is still running, into its worn stone basin, as it does all night, every night.

In a small town by the sea, the walls are covered in writing.

And if you walk its streets today, and stop at a doorway, and look down... you may find one word, set into the floor in small stones, to greet whoever comes in. Romans said it to each other on the street, every day. They wrote it on their walls. They put it on their doorsteps.

Salve. It means... be well.
